"Same building block, different linkage geometry, different molecule" (starch versus cellulose) is familiar ground. The sharper version examiners reach for: even identical building blocks joined by the identical KIND of linkage can still differ once branching enters the picture.

Counterexample: glycogen and amylopectin (a component of starch) are both branched glucose polymers, both using the same coiled glycosidic linkage geometry as ordinary starch, and both additionally carrying branch points. Yet glycogen is far more densely branched than amylopectin. That extra branching is not a different kind of bond at all — it is the same linkage type occurring more frequently, at shorter intervals along the chain — and it is precisely what makes glycogen the faster-mobilised energy store (more branch ends means more places enzymes can act on simultaneously) compared to starch's slower release. "Same linkage type" settles far less than it appears to; frequency and pattern of that linkage carry real, separate information.
