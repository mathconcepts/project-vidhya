Sketch a snapshot of a wave on a string at one instant: a repeating up-down pattern along its length. The distance from one crest to the next crest is the **wavelength** $\lambda$ (in metres). Now watch one fixed point on the string over time instead — it goes up and down, up and down, completing one full up-down cycle in a time called the **time period** $T$ (seconds), or equivalently, $f=1/T$ full cycles every second (the **frequency**, in hertz). The wave equation ties the two pictures together: $v=f\lambda$, where $v$ (m/s) is how fast the pattern itself moves along the string.

The wave's speed $v$ is set entirely by the *medium* it travels through, not by how the wave was created: on a stretched string, $v=\sqrt{T_s/\mu}$ ($T_s$ = tension in newtons, $\mu$ = mass per unit length in kg/m); in a gas, $v=\sqrt{\gamma P/\rho}$ ($P$ = pressure, $\rho$ = density). Changing how hard or how often you shake the string changes the wave's frequency, never its speed — speed only changes if the string itself (its tension or its mass per length) changes.

Two waves travelling in opposite directions along the same string add up, point by point, and can produce a **standing wave**: a pattern that no longer travels at all, with fixed points (**nodes**) that never move and points in between (**antinodes**) that swing with the largest amplitude. Two waves of slightly different frequency overlapping instead produce **beats** — a slow rise and fall in loudness, at frequency $|f_1-f_2|$, as the two waves drift in and out of step with each other.

Finally, if the *source* of the wave (or the listener) is moving relative to the medium, the frequency actually heard shifts — higher when source and listener approach each other, lower when they move apart. This is the **Doppler effect**, and it changes only the perceived frequency, never the wave's true speed through the medium.
