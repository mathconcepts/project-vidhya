Take one real molecule: 2-bromo-3-methylbutane, $\text{CH}_3\text{-CHBr-CH(CH}_3\text{)-CH}_3$. Carbon 2 carries the bromine and is joined to two other carbons — a **secondary carbon**.

Step 1: the bond holding bromine snaps, bromide ion leaves, and carbon 2 becomes a **carbocation** — three bonds, an empty orbital, a plus charge.

Step 2: look at carbon 3, right next door. It already carries three carbon groups (a **tertiary** position) plus one hydrogen.

Step 3: that hydrogen, with its whole bonding electron pair, slides over from carbon 3 to carbon 2's empty orbital. The plus charge is now on carbon 3.

Check: carbon 3 is now bonded to three carbons and nothing else — more stable than the secondary carbon it replaced. The move happened because the new spot is genuinely more comfortable, not because anything forced it.
