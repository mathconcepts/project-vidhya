Every biomolecule in this concept is built the same general way: small, repeating units joined end to end by losing a small molecule (usually water) at each join. The restriction the school course sometimes skips over is that the SAME kind of link — a sugar-to-sugar bond called a **glycosidic linkage**, or an amino-acid-to-amino-acid bond called a **peptide bond** — can connect its units in more than one way, and the way they connect changes the whole molecule's properties.

Take starch and cellulose. Both are made of nothing but glucose units, joined by glycosidic linkages. Starch's units are linked so the chain coils up, which the human digestive system can break apart for energy. Cellulose's units are linked in a different geometric arrangement, giving straight, tightly packed chains that the same digestive enzymes cannot break down at all — which is why humans can digest starch (rice, bread) but not cellulose (the fibrous, indigestible part of plants).

Same building block, same general kind of bond, different linkage geometry, completely different biological outcome. Checking "which glucose units, and how exactly are they linked" — not just "is this made of glucose?" — is what actually distinguishes one glucose-based polysaccharide from another.
