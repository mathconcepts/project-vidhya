A metal plate sits under a lamp. Turn the brightness up as high as you like — the electrons it knocks loose never fly out any faster. Turn up the light's colour instead, from red toward violet, and suddenly they fly out much faster, even at low brightness.

This is the **photoelectric effect**: light hitting a metal surface knocks electrons out of it (called photoelectrons). Wave theory said a brighter (more intense) wave carries more energy, so brighter light should always mean faster electrons. It does not — only the light's **frequency** (how many wave cycles pass a point each second) decides the electrons' top speed. Brightness only decides how many electrons come out.

Einstein's fix: treat light as a stream of **photons**, discrete packets of energy of size $E=hf$, where $h$ is Planck's constant (a fixed tiny number) and $f$ is frequency. One photon hits one electron; a higher-frequency photon gives a bigger kick, whatever the brightness.
