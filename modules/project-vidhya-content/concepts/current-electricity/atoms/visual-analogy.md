Picture a water pump lifting water to a height, then letting it flow back down through a network of pipes before returning to the pump. The pump is the **cell** — it does work lifting the water (its emf is the maximum height it *could* lift water to, with nothing else draining its effort). A narrow section of pipe right at the pump's own outlet, which the water has to squeeze through even before reaching the rest of the network, is the cell's **internal resistance** — some of the pump's own effort is used up right there, before any water even leaves the pump housing. Whatever height is actually left over once that narrow squeeze is accounted for is the **terminal voltage** — always a little less than what the pump could ideally deliver.

Downstream, the water splits at junctions and rejoins later — exactly Kirchhoff's junction rule (whatever flows in at a junction must flow back out), and the total height dropped going around any complete loop of pipes and back to the pump must add up to exactly what the pump lifted, which is Kirchhoff's loop rule.
