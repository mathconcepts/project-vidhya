Formalin — the liquid biology labs use to preserve specimens — is just formaldehyde ($\text{HCHO}$) dissolved in water. It reacts with almost anything nearby, including the proteins in living tissue, which is exactly why it preserves them. Acetone, the main ingredient in nail-polish remover, carries the identical reactive group but reacts far more slowly with the same kind of attacking species (a nucleophile — an electron-rich atom or ion that seeks out a positive centre to bond with).

Both molecules have a carbon double-bonded to oxygen: the **carbonyl group**. The oxygen pulls shared electrons towards itself (it is more electronegative — better at hogging bonding electrons), leaving the carbon short of electron density and open to attack. So why does one carbonyl compound react so much faster than the other?

The answer is what sits on either side of that carbon. Formaldehyde's carbonyl carbon carries only two small hydrogens. Acetone's carries two bulky methyl groups instead. Those methyl groups physically crowd the approach path (a steric effect) and also push extra electron density onto the carbon (an electronic effect), both of which make it a harder, less willing target. The more alkyl groups crowd the carbonyl carbon, the slower this whole family of reactions goes — one idea that ranks every aldehyde and ketone at once.
