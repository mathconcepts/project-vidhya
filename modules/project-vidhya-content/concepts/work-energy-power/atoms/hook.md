A fast bowler's arm speeds a $0.16\text{ kg}$ cricket ball from rest to $30\text{ m/s}$ over an arm-swing of about $1.5\text{ m}$. The ball's final kinetic energy (energy of motion, $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$) is $\tfrac{1}{2}(0.16)(30)^2 = 72\text{ J}$ (joules, the SI unit of energy and work). Since it started at rest, all of that $72\text{ J}$ was **work** done on the ball by the arm.

Divide that work by the distance, $72/1.5 = 48\text{ N}$, and you get the average force the arm applied — a number "how hard the bowler swung" can't give you directly. This is the **work-energy theorem**: net work done on an object always equals its change in kinetic energy, no matter how twisty the force actually was along the way. **Power** is a second, separate question — the same $72\text{ J}$ delivered in $0.1\text{ s}$ is a very different bowler from one who takes $0.3\text{ s}$, even though the work done is identical.
