Take one negative charge, sitting alone. A positive test charge placed $1\ \text{cm}$ to its right feels a pull **towards** the negative charge, i.e. an arrow pointing left, into it. That is the electric field's direction there — always the direction a *positive* test charge would be pushed. Near a negative source, that direction is inward, not outward.

Now: does Gauss's law help find the field of a uniformly charged sphere? Yes — a sphere is symmetric, so $E$ is the same size everywhere on an imaginary sphere drawn around it, and $\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A = Q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$ becomes one equation, one unknown.

Does it help find the field from two separate point charges sitting apart? No — no symmetric surface exists to draw around both at once. Here, add up each charge's own Coulomb's-law field separately (superposition) instead.
