**Cross-concept check: dual-nature-matter → atoms-and-nuclei.**

**Question 1 (ionization energy feeds a photon wavelength):** Hydrogen's ground-state energy is $E_1=-13.6$ eV. Find the **ionization energy** (energy needed to free the electron completely, sending it to $n=\infty$ where $E_\infty=0$), then the wavelength of a photon carrying exactly that much energy.

*Answer:* Ionization energy $=E_\infty-E_1=0-(-13.6)=13.6$ eV. Using $E\approx1240/\lambda(\text{nm})$ (a photon-energy relation from dual-nature-matter): $\lambda=1240/13.6\approx91.2$ nm — this is the **Lyman series limit**, the shortest wavelength that series ever reaches.

**Question 2 (a specific transition's photon, checked the same way):** An electron drops from $n=3$ to $n=1$. Find the released photon's energy and wavelength.

*Answer:* $E_3-E_1=-1.51-(-13.6)=12.09$ eV. Wavelength: $\lambda=1240/12.09\approx102.6$ nm — noticeably longer than the Lyman limit above, since this transition releases *less* energy than a full ionization-to-ground-state jump.

**Why this drill exists:** the Bohr model's energy-level arithmetic and the photon-energy formula from dual-nature-matter are the same $E=hf\approx1240/\lambda(\text{nm})$ relation, applied twice — once to find how much energy a jump releases, once to turn that energy into a wavelength a spectrometer would actually measure.
