The single-plane case (elevation $30°$ then $60°$ after closing $20$ m) solves to $h=10\sqrt3\approx17.32$ m by equating $d/\sqrt3=(d-20)\sqrt3$, giving $d=30$. The genuinely testable idea is what changes when the SECOND observation is not along the same line but along a perpendicular direction instead — the two-vertical-planes case, where $c^2=p^2+q^2$ replaces simple substitution.

Take a tower of height $10$ m observed at $45°$ due east ($p=10\cot45°=10$) and at $30°$ due north ($q=10\cot30°=10\sqrt3\approx17.32$). The straight-line ground distance between the two observation points is $c=\sqrt{p^2+q^2}=\sqrt{100+300}=\sqrt{400}=20$ m — found by Pythagoras on the two horizontal legs, NOT by adding or subtracting $p$ and $q$ directly ($p+q\approx27.32$, a completely different, wrong number). The perpendicularity between the two directions is what licenses treating $p$ and $q$ as legs of a right triangle in the first place; without it, $c^2=p^2+q^2$ is simply not the correct relationship to use.
