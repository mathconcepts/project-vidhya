**Problem:** Cyclopentadiene loses a proton to give $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5^-$ or a hydride to give $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5^+$. The anion has $6$ pi electrons ($4n+2$, $n=1$) — aromatic. The cation has $4$ ($4n$, $n=1$) — antiaromatic.

$$\boxed{\text{losing a proton (anion) is aromatic; losing a hydride (cation) is antiaromatic, here}}$$

---

**"Cations are antiaromatic" is the overreach.** Try the same hydride-loss move on a seven-membered ring instead: cycloheptatriene has three double bonds ($6$ pi electrons); losing a hydride from its one $\text{CH}_2$ carbon leaves an empty p-orbital, contributing nothing — still $6$ total. Check $4n+2=6$: $n=1$, a whole number. The **tropylium cation**, $\text{C}_7\text{H}_7^+$, is aromatic — formed by the identical move (hydride loss from a ring's one non-conjugated carbon) that made the five-membered case antiaromatic.

The charge sign never decides aromaticity on its own. Only the resulting electron count, checked against $4n+2$ for that specific ring, does — and the same operation on two different ring sizes can land on opposite sides of the rule.
