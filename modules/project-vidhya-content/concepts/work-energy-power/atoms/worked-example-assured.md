**Problem:** A $2\text{ kg}$ block at $6\text{ m/s}$ on a rough floor ($\mu=0.3$) is stopped by friction alone. Find the stopping distance using the work-energy theorem. Take $g=10\text{ m/s}^2$.

---

**Step 0 — Set up before any formula.** Floor frame, direction of motion positive, friction's force and work both negative — the one force doing work here is friction, so it carries the whole energy account.

---

**Step 1 — Skip the acceleration entirely.** $f = \mu mg = 6\text{ N}$, $KE_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(2)(6)^2 = 36\text{ J}$, $KE_f=0$.

$$-fd = 0 - 36 \implies d = \frac{36}{6} = 6\text{ m}$$

$$\boxed{d = 6\text{ m}}$$

---

**Why the energy route beats kinematics here.** Kinematics needs the acceleration computed first ($a=f/m$) and then a $v^2=u^2-2ad$ equation solved for $d$ — two steps of algebra hiding one physical fact: *all* the kinetic energy became friction's work, nothing else. The instant a problem asks "how far/how fast" without caring about time, and only one force does work, the energy equation is a straight substitution — no acceleration required at all. Reach for kinematics only when time is explicitly part of the question, or when more than one force makes the energy account genuinely ambiguous without it.
