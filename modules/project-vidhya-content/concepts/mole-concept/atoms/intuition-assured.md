The gap that costs marks: **empirical formula** is not the same thing as **molecular formula**, and "moles of atoms" is not the same thing as "moles of molecules".

A compound found to be 40% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen, 53.3% oxygen by mass gives mole ratios of atoms $\approx 1:2:1$, so its empirical formula is $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ — the *simplest whole-number ratio*, nothing more. Its actual molecular formula could be $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ itself, or $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_2$, or $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ — any whole multiple of the empirical formula. Percentage composition alone can never distinguish between them; only a separately measured **molecular mass** fixes the multiplier $n = \dfrac{\text{molecular mass}}{\text{empirical formula mass}}$.

A second trap: 1 mole of $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ contains 1 mole of *molecules*, but 4 moles of *atoms* (1 C + 2 H + 1 O). A question asking for "moles of hydrogen atoms" is not answered by the moles of the compound.
