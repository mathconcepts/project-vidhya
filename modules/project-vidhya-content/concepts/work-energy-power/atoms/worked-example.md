**Problem:** A $2\text{ kg}$ block slides on a rough horizontal floor at $6\text{ m/s}$, then decelerates under friction alone ($\mu = 0.3$) until it stops. Using the work-energy theorem (not kinematics equations), find the distance it travels before stopping. Take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.

---

**Step 0 — Set up before any formula.** Physical situation: a block sliding along a horizontal floor, decelerating only because of friction (no other horizontal force). Frame of reference: the floor, with the block's direction of motion taken as **positive**. Sign convention: friction always opposes motion, so its force and its work are both **negative** in this frame.

---

**Step 1 — Find the friction force.** $f = \mu mg = (0.3)(2)(10) = 6\text{ N}$, directed opposite to motion (negative, by the sign convention above).

---

**Step 2 — Find the initial kinetic energy.** $KE_i = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(2)(6)^2 = 36\text{ J}$.

---

**Step 3 — Apply the work-energy theorem.** The block stops, so $KE_f = 0$. The only force doing work is friction: $W_{friction} = KE_f - KE_i = 0 - 36 = -36\text{ J}$.

---

**Step 4 — Solve for distance.** $W_{friction} = -f \cdot d$ (negative, matching the sign convention), so $-36 = -6d \Rightarrow d = 6\text{ m}$.

---

**Step 5 — State the answer.** $\boxed{d = 6\text{ m}}$ — reached without ever writing down an acceleration or a $v^2=u^2+2as$ equation; the energy account settles it directly.
