Everyone already knows $W_{net} = \Delta KE$. What actually separates a confident scorer here: this theorem is **unconditionally true**, while "mechanical energy is conserved" is **conditionally true**, and treating the two as interchangeable is where marks go.

Counterexample: a $2\text{ kg}$ block slides down a rough incline. Gravity does positive work, friction does negative work, and the work-energy theorem still holds exactly — net work equals the block's kinetic-energy gain, full stop, friction included. But mechanical energy ($KE+PE$) is *not* conserved here, because friction (a non-conservative force) does non-zero work; some gravitational PE converts to heat instead of kinetic energy. A student who writes $KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f$ for this block has silently assumed a frictionless world that the problem never granted. The work-energy theorem never needs that assumption — it is the one law you can trust even when friction, drag, or an applied push are in play.
