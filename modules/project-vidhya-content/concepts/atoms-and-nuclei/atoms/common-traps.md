- **Treating a spectral line as if it came from a single orbit**: a line's colour is fixed by the *gap* between two energy levels, $E_{\text{photon}}=E_{n_{\text{high}}}-E_{n_{\text{low}}}$, released only at the instant of a jump. A single stationary orbit, with no jump happening, emits nothing at all — "which orbit is this light from" is not even a question with an answer.

- **Reading "after two half-lives" as "fully decayed"**: after one half-life, half the sample is left; after two, it is a *quarter* left ($\left(\tfrac12\right)^2$), never zero. A radioactive sample only ever approaches zero, it never reaches it after any finite number of half-lives.

- **Using atomic number $Z$ instead of mass number $A$ in the nuclear-size formula**: $R=R_0A^{1/3}$ needs $A$ (protons *plus* neutrons together), not $Z$ (protons alone) — swapping the two silently shrinks the computed radius for every nucleus with more neutrons than protons.

- **Mixing bare-proton mass with hydrogen-atom mass mid-calculation**: binding-energy sums must use *either* bare nuclear masses throughout *or* atomic masses (which already include electrons) throughout — mixing the two leaves a leftover electron mass uncancelled, quietly shifting the answer.

- **Assuming any fusion or any fission releases energy**: only a change that moves nucleons *toward* the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve's peak (around iron, $A\approx56$) releases energy. Fusing nuclei already near the peak into something heavier, or splitting a light nucleus into individual nucleons, both take energy in instead.
