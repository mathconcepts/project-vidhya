The calculation above silently assumed the solute never dissociates or associates — worth checking, since assuming $i=1$ when it is not equal to $1$ produces a plausible-looking but wrong molar mass.

If this same $1.05\ \text{g}$ sample had instead been a weak electrolyte with $i=1.5$, the SAME $\Delta T_f=1.2\ \text{K}$ would correspond to fewer actual formula units and more actual particles — so the "molar mass" computed by ignoring $i$ ($172.3\ \text{g/mol}$ above) would be the TRUE molar mass divided by $i$: $M_{\text{true}} = i \times M_{\text{apparent}} = 1.5 \times 172.3 \approx 258.5\ \text{g/mol}$.

**A colligative-property molar-mass calculation always finds mass per PARTICLE, not mass per formula unit — the two are identical only when $i=1$.** Whenever a solute might ionise or associate, $i$ must be found (or given) before trusting any molar mass pulled straight out of $\Delta T_f$, $\Delta T_b$, or $\Pi$.
