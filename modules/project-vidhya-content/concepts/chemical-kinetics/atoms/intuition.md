The **rate of a reaction** is how fast a reactant's concentration falls (or a product's rises) with time. A **rate law** writes this as $\text{rate}=k[A]^m[B]^n$ — an equation found by EXPERIMENT, not by looking at the reaction's balanced equation. The exponents $m$ and $n$ together give the **order** of the reaction; $k$ is the **rate constant**, a fixed number (at a given temperature) that sets how fast the reaction runs once concentrations are known.

**Molecularity** is a different, mechanistic idea: it counts how many molecules actually come together and collide in one single reaction step (an "elementary step" — one of possibly several steps making up the full mechanism). Molecularity is always a small whole number, because there is no such thing as "half a molecule" colliding. Order, in contrast, comes purely from measurement, and matches molecularity only by coincidence, for a reaction that genuinely happens in one single step.

For a **first-order** reaction, $[A]$ falls off as $[A]=[A]_0e^{-kt}$ — an exponential decay curve. Its **half-life**, $t_{1/2}=\dfrac{\ln 2}{k}\approx\dfrac{0.693}{k}$, is a constant, independent of the starting concentration — exactly the "halving time" pattern a drug's elimination from the bloodstream shows.
