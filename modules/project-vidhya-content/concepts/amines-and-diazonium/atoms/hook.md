Bright red and orange dyes used in fabric colouring are often **azo dyes** — molecules containing an $\text{-N=N-}$ link between two ring systems. That link is built in the lab by reacting a **diazonium salt** (from a primary aromatic amine treated with cold nitrous acid) with a second aromatic ring — but only if that second ring is "activated": carrying a strong electron-donating group like $\text{-OH}$ or $\text{-NH}_2$. Try the same coupling reaction on an ordinary, unactivated benzene ring and nothing happens at all.

The reason traces back to how weak an electrophile the diazonium ion actually is. It needs a ring with extra electron density already pushed into it — exactly what an $\text{-OH}$ or $\text{-NH}_2$ group supplies — to have any chance of reacting. Everything else in this concept, from how basic an amine is in water to how that amine gets made in the first place, comes down to the same idea: how much (or how little) electron density is available, and where it sits.
