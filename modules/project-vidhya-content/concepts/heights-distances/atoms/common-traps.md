- **Elevation-depression sign confusion**: the angle of depression from the TOP is measured from the observer's OWN horizontal, not from the vertical drop straight down — students sometimes subtract it from $90°$ by mistake before using it.
- **Swapping opposite and adjacent**: in $\tan\theta=h/d$, height is always opposite the angle and horizontal distance is always adjacent — using $\cot\theta$ where $\tan\theta$ belongs (or vice versa) silently inverts the entire answer.
- **Using the horizontal-distance formula when the line of sight is given**: if a problem states the length of a ladder, a kite's string, or a rope directly, that length is the hypotenuse, and $\sin\theta$ (not $\tan\theta$) is the ratio that applies.
- **Adding the two ground distances instead of using Pythagoras**: in a two-vertical-planes problem, the ground distance between two perpendicular observation points is $\sqrt{p^2+q^2}$, never $p+q$ — these differ by a large margin except in degenerate cases.
- **Leaving the observer's own height out**: some problems give the observer's eye-level height above the ground explicitly. Forgetting to add it to (or subtract it from) the object's height above the observer is a frequent, easy-to-miss one-mark loss.
