**Oxidation** is losing electrons; **reduction** is gaining them — and in any real chemical reaction, both always happen together, since electrons lost by one species have to go somewhere. A **redox reaction** is simply a reaction where this electron transfer happens; balancing one means tracking exactly how many electrons move, using **oxidation numbers** (a bookkeeping charge assigned to each atom, as if every bond were fully ionic) to spot which atoms gained or lost electrons.

An **electrochemical cell** separates the two halves of a redox reaction physically, forcing the electrons to travel through an external wire instead of jumping directly between atoms — and that flow of electrons through the wire is exactly what a current meter reads as electricity. The **anode** is where oxidation happens (electrons are released here); the **cathode** is where reduction happens (electrons are consumed here) — this naming never changes, in any electrochemical cell of any kind.

A **standard electrode potential** $E^\circ$ measures how strongly a half-reaction pulls towards reduction, against a fixed reference (hydrogen's own half-reaction, defined as exactly $0\ \text{V}$). A more positive $E^\circ$ means a stronger pull towards gaining electrons.
