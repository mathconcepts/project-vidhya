**Cross-concept check: biomolecules → polymers.**

**Question 1 (proteins are natural condensation polymers):** A peptide bond forms between two amino acids with the loss of one water molecule per bond. Is a protein, chemically speaking, an addition polymer or a condensation polymer?

*Answer:* A **condensation polymer**. Its monomers (amino acids) join with the loss of a small molecule — water — at every single bond, exactly the same defining feature the next concept uses to classify synthetic polymers like nylon. A protein is simply a naturally occurring condensation polymer, built from a much larger and more varied set of monomers (twenty different amino acids) than any one synthetic condensation polymer usually uses.

**Question 2 (starch as a natural polymer, contrasted with a synthetic one):** Starch is built from repeating glucose units joined by glycosidic linkages, with no small molecule released once the chain is drawn as a whole. Is starch an addition polymer for the same reason polythene is?

*Answer:* No. Polythene's monomer, ethylene, has no water or other small molecule to lose — its double bond simply opens and monomers link directly, which is what makes it a true addition polymer. Starch's glycosidic linkage DOES release a water molecule at each individual join; it is a condensation polymer, like a protein, even though the overall chain, once finished, does not obviously advertise a lost molecule the way the equation for its formation does step by step.

**Why this drill exists:** the loss-of-a-small-molecule test this concept applies to peptide bonds and glycosidic linkages is the exact same test the next concept uses to sort every synthetic polymer into addition or condensation.
