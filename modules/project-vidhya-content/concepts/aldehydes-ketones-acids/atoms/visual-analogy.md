Picture a doorway with people trying to squeeze through it from one side — the nucleophile approaching the carbonyl carbon from the opposite side to the $\text{C=O}$ bond. Formaldehyde's carbonyl carbon has only two hydrogens standing either side of that doorway — thin, out of the way, barely blocking anything. Acetone's carbonyl carbon has two bulky methyl groups standing there instead, like two people with backpacks crowding the same doorway. The nucleophile has to squeeze past far more bulk to reach acetone's carbonyl carbon than formaldehyde's — and this crowding is exactly the steric effect that slows nucleophilic addition as more alkyl groups pile onto the carbonyl carbon.

There is a second, invisible effect layered on top of the crowding, and this is where the analogy needs a second picture: each of those backpack-wearing methyl groups is also quietly donating electron density into the carbonyl carbon through the bond connecting them, the way a tap left slightly open keeps topping up a tank. A carbon that already has more electron density to start with is a less attractive, less electron-hungry target for an incoming nucleophile — so acetone's carbonyl carbon is not just physically harder to reach, it is also less eager to be attacked in the first place. Reactivity depends on both pictures together, not either alone.
