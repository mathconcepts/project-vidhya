Dissolve $0.1\ \text{mol}$ of glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$, which does not split into ions) in $1\ \text{kg}$ of water. $\Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m$, with $K_f(\text{water})=1.86\ \text{K kg/mol}$, $m=0.1\ \text{mol/kg}$, and $i=1$ (one particle stays one particle): $\Delta T_f = 1 \times 1.86 \times 0.1 = 0.186\ \text{K}$.

Now dissolve $0.1\ \text{mol}$ of $\text{NaCl}$ in the same $1\ \text{kg}$ of water instead. $\text{NaCl}$ splits fully into $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$, so $i=2$: $\Delta T_f = 2 \times 1.86 \times 0.1 = 0.372\ \text{K}$.

Check: same NUMBER of moles dissolved, same solvent, same $K_f$ — yet $\text{NaCl}$'s freezing-point drop is exactly double glucose's, because it puts twice as many particles into solution per mole. The identity of the solute barely mattered; the PARTICLE COUNT decided the answer.
