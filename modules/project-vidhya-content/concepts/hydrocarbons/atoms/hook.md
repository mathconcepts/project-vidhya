Draw benzene as three alternating single and double bonds in a ring, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$, and every ordinary rule for a double bond says it should react the way cyclohexene does — fast addition with bromine water, decolourising it almost instantly. Benzene does not. It sits there, barely reacting with bromine water at all, and when it finally does react with bromine (needing a catalyst), it swaps out a hydrogen instead of adding across a double bond.

Something about this particular ring of six carbons is unusually settled — far more settled than three ordinary double bonds stitched together would predict. That extra, measurable calm is called **aromaticity**, and one number-based rule, **Huckel's rule**, tells you in advance, just by counting electrons, whether a given ring will show it or not.
