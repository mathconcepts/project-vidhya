**$S_N2$** (substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular): the nucleophile's lone pair attacks the carbon from the side directly opposite the leaving halogen, as the carbon-halogen bond breaks at the exact same moment — one single step, both events at once. Picture an umbrella flipping inside out in a strong wind: the carbon's three other groups swing from one side to the other as the attack happens, so the product's arrangement in space is the mirror image of the starting one (**inversion of configuration**). This backside approach needs open space behind the carbon, so it works best on a **primary** carbon and gets progressively harder as more groups crowd around it.

**$S_N1$** (substitution, nucleophilic, unimolecular): the halogen leaves *first*, entirely on its own, taking the bonding electron pair with it and leaving behind a flat carbocation. Only afterwards does the nucleophile's lone pair attack — and because the carbocation is flat, it can be attacked equally from either face, giving a roughly equal mix of both spatial arrangements (**racemisation**). This route depends on the carbocation being stable enough to form in the first place, so it favours a **tertiary** carbon, where hyperconjugation and the inductive effect from three neighbouring alkyl groups hold the positive charge comfortably.

A **secondary** carbon sits in between and can go either way, depending on which reagent and solvent are actually present.
