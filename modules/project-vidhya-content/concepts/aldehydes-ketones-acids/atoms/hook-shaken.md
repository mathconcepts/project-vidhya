Take two real molecules: formaldehyde ($\text{HCHO}$) and acetone ($\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3$). Both have a carbon double-bonded to oxygen — the **carbonyl group**. Oxygen is more electronegative (it pulls shared electrons towards itself harder than carbon does), so that carbon sits short of electrons and open to attack by a nucleophile (an electron-rich species that bonds to a positive centre).

Count what's attached to the carbonyl carbon in each. Formaldehyde: two hydrogens, nothing else. Acetone: two methyl groups instead.

Two methyl groups do two jobs at once. First, they physically sit in the way of an incoming nucleophile (a steric effect) — one methyl group blocks less space than two. Second, alkyl groups push electron density onto the carbon they're attached to, which partly cancels the carbonyl carbon's electron shortage (an electronic effect) and makes it less attractive to attack.

Formaldehyde has zero alkyl groups on its carbonyl carbon. Acetone has two. Zero crowding, zero electron push, versus two of each — that gap alone is why formalin reacts fast enough to preserve tissue while nail-polish remover barely reacts with the same nucleophiles.
