The **work-energy theorem** says: net work done by *all* forces on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy, $W_{net} = \Delta KE$. This holds no matter how many separate forces are pushing, pulling or dragging — add up every force's individual work (a force's work is $W = Fd\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement), and that total equals $KE_{final} - KE_{initial}$, always.

A block pushed by a $10\text{ N}$ force across $4\text{ m}$ of rough floor, against a $6\text{ N}$ friction force, gains net work $10(4) + (-6)(4) = 40 - 24 = 16\text{ J}$ — friction's work is negative because friction always points opposite the motion. That $16\text{ J}$, not the $40\text{ J}$ from the push alone, is what actually shows up as extra kinetic energy.

**Mechanical energy** ($KE + PE$) is a stricter, more fragile idea: it stays constant only when the net work done by *non-conservative* forces (friction, air drag, an applied push) is zero. The block above has friction acting, so its mechanical energy is *not* conserved — some of the push's $40\text{ J}$ leaks away as heat at the sliding surface, even though the work-energy theorem still correctly accounts for all $16\text{ J}$ of real kinetic-energy gain.
