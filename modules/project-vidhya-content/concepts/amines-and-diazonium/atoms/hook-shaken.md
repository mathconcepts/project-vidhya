Take one real diazonium salt: benzenediazonium chloride, made by treating aniline ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2$) with cold nitrous acid.

Mix it with phenol ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH}$). A bright orange-red azo dye forms — a new $\text{-N=N-}$ link between the two rings, right at phenol's ring carbon opposite the $\text{-OH}$ group.

Now mix the same diazonium salt with plain benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$) instead. Nothing happens.

The difference is the $\text{-OH}$ group. It pushes extra electron density into phenol's ring, making that ring electron-rich enough for the weak diazonium electrophile to attack. Plain benzene has no such group and no extra electron density to offer, so the coupling never starts. One substituent, present or absent, decides whether this reaction runs at all.
