Take one real ion: $Mn^{2+}$, configuration $3d^5$. Five electrons, five separate $d$ orbitals — every single one sits alone, unpaired. Check: $Mn^{2+}$ compounds are pale pink (a real colour, even if faint) and are strongly attracted to a magnet (paramagnetic).

Now compare $Zn^{2+}$, configuration $3d^{10}$: all ten $d$ electrons are paired up, none left alone. $Zn^{2+}$ compounds are colourless and are not attracted to a magnet at all.

The pattern: unpaired $d$ electrons are what make colour and magnetism happen at all. Colour needs an empty $d$ orbital nearby for an electron to jump into when it absorbs light — $Zn^{2+}$'s fully paired, fully occupied subshell has nowhere to jump. Magnetism needs unpaired electrons, since each one acts like a tiny magnet — $Zn^{2+}$ has none.

Variable oxidation states follow the same logic from a different angle: $d$ and $s$ electrons sit close enough in energy that different numbers of $d$ electrons can join bonding depending on the situation. $Mn$ shows oxidation states from $+2$ all the way to $+7$, because its five $3d$ electrons plus two $4s$ electrons are all close enough in energy to be pulled into bonding, one at a time or all together, as a given reaction demands.
