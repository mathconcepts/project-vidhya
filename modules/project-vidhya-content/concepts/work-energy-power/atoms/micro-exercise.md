A $1\text{ kg}$ block moving at $4\text{ m/s}$ on a rough floor is brought to rest by friction alone over a distance of $2\text{ m}$. What is the magnitude of the friction force?

A) $2\text{ N}$
B) $4\text{ N}$
C) $8\text{ N}$
D) $16\text{ N}$
E) Cannot be found without the coefficient of friction

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct answer: B) $4\text{ N}$.**

Initial kinetic energy: $KE_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(1)(4)^2 = 8\text{ J}$. Final kinetic energy: $KE_f = 0$ (block stops). By the work-energy theorem, friction's work is $W_f = KE_f - KE_i = 0 - 8 = -8\text{ J}$. Since $W_f = -f \cdot d$ (friction opposes motion), $-8 = -f(2) \Rightarrow f = 4\text{ N}$.

**Why the others fail:**
- A) $2\text{ N}$ comes from dividing $KE_i$ by distance without doubling it first — treating $8/2$ as if $8\text{ J}$ were already the answer to a different, unrelated question.
- C) $8\text{ N}$ mistakes the kinetic energy value itself, $8\text{ J}$, for the force.
- D) $16\text{ N}$ comes from forgetting the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in $KE=\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$, using $mv^2=16$ directly as the kinetic energy.
- E) is wrong because the work-energy theorem finds the force directly from energy and distance — the coefficient of friction is never needed at all.

</details>
