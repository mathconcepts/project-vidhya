**Dynamic equilibrium** does not mean "the reaction has stopped" — it means the forward and reverse reactions are both still running, at *equal rates*, so concentrations stop visibly changing while molecules keep converting back and forth underneath.

**$Q$ versus $K$** is the single most useful comparison here. $K$ (the equilibrium constant, $K_c$ using concentrations or $K_p$ using partial pressures) is the fixed value the ratio of products to reactants settles to at a given temperature. $Q$ (the reaction quotient) uses that exact same expression but can be computed at *any* moment. Comparing them tells the whole story: $Q<K$ means the forward reaction still has room to run (shifts toward products); $Q>K$ means the reverse reaction runs instead (shifts toward reactants); $Q=K$ means the system is already at equilibrium, with no further net shift.

**Le Chatelier's Principle** predicts the *direction* of that shift when a stress (added reactant, removed product, pressure or volume change, temperature change) disturbs an equilibrium — a prediction rule about which way the balance tips, not an explanation of the underlying rates.

A **catalyst** changes neither $Q$'s value at a given instant nor $K$ itself — it only shortens the time taken to reach the same eventual balance.
