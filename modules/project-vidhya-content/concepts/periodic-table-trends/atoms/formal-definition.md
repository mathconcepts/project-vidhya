**Modern periodic law**: properties of elements repeat periodically with atomic number (the number of protons), not atomic mass.

**Blocks**: elements are grouped by which subshell receives the last electron added — $s$-block (groups 1–2), $p$-block (groups 13–18), $d$-block (groups 3–12, the transition elements), $f$-block (lanthanoids and actinoids, placed below the main table).

**Atomic radius**: distance from the nucleus to the outermost electron shell, usually measured as covalent radius (half the distance between two identical bonded atoms). Shrinks across a period (effective nuclear charge rises, same shell), grows down a group (a whole new shell is added each step).

**Ionic radius**: a cation (positive ion) is smaller than its parent atom — fewer electrons pull each other apart less, and losing a whole outer shell shrinks it further. An anion (negative ion) is larger than its parent atom — one extra electron increases electron-electron repulsion.

**Ionisation enthalpy ($IE_1$)**: energy needed to remove the outermost electron from one mole of isolated gaseous atoms. Rises across a period, falls down a group — with two named exceptions per period: from a filled subshell to the next (e.g. $2s^2 \to 2s^22p^1$) and from a half-filled subshell to a paired one (e.g. $2p^3 \to 2p^4$). $IE_2$ (removing a second electron) is always greater than $IE_1$, since the electron is now being pulled off an already positive ion.

**Electron gain enthalpy**: energy change when one mole of gaseous atoms each gains one electron. Generally becomes more negative across a period and less negative down a group, but oxygen and fluorine — the smallest members of their groups — have a *less* negative value than the element directly below them (sulfur, chlorine), because the incoming electron is forced into an already compact, electron-dense $2p$ subshell and faces extra repulsion there.

**Electronegativity**: how strongly a bonded atom pulls a shared pair of electrons towards itself, measured on the Pauling scale (fluorine highest, at 4.0). Rises across a period, falls down a group.

**Diagonal relationship**: each of Li, Be, B (Period 2) resembles the element one group to its right and one period down (Mg, Al, Si respectively) more closely than it resembles the heavier members of its own group — both elements in a diagonal pair have a similar charge-to-radius ratio, and that ratio, not the group number, drives much of an element's chemistry.
