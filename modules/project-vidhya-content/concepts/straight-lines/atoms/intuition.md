Call the crossing point of $L_1=0$ and $L_2=0$ point $P$. At $P$, both $L_1$ and $L_2$ come out to $0$. So for ANY number $\lambda$, plugging $P$ into $L_1+\lambda L_2$ gives $0+\lambda(0)=0$ — meaning $P$ satisfies $L_1+\lambda L_2=0$ too, no matter what $\lambda$ is. And since $L_1+\lambda L_2=0$ is still a straight line for each fixed $\lambda$, every value of $\lambda$ hands you a different line through the SAME point $P$.

The trick, then: you never solve $L_1=0$ and $L_2=0$ together to find $P$. You pick $\lambda$ to satisfy one extra condition — passing through a given point, parallel to a given line, perpendicular to one — and $P$ rides along for free.

You already have angle between two lines from their slopes: $\tan\theta=\left|\dfrac{m_1-m_2}{1+m_1m_2}\right|$. Family of lines and angle between lines are the same toolkit — one gives a line through a fixed point, the other says how two lines lean against each other.
