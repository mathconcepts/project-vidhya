The mechanics of the inert pair effect are assumed known here — the mark-costing trap is assuming the SAME group always reacts in the same direction just because "the inert pair effect applies to both."

Counterexample, same group, two neighbouring elements: $SnCl_2$ is a reducing agent — it readily gives up electrons and turns into $Sn^{4+}$, because for tin, $+4$ is the more comfortable state. $PbO_2$ behaves the opposite way — it readily grabs electrons and drops to $Pb^{2+}$, because for lead, $+2$ is the more comfortable state. Same effect, same group, two elements apart, and the direction of "which state is unstable" completely flips. The inert pair effect is not a fixed switch that is either "on" or "off" for a group — its strength grows steadily down the group, and exactly where it crosses over from "higher state favoured" to "lower state favoured" is itself the fact worth memorising, not just that the effect exists somewhere in Group 14.
