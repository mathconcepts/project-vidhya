**Cross-concept check: wave-optics → electromagnetic-waves.**

**Question 1 (what a polariser is actually restricting):** An electromagnetic wave has its electric field oscillating perpendicular to its direction of travel. Unpolarised light has this electric field oscillating in *every* perpendicular direction at once, randomly. What exactly does a polariser do to it?

*Answer:* A polariser only lets through the component of the electric field oscillating along one particular direction (its transmission axis), blocking the rest. "Polarised light" is simply light whose electric field has been restricted to oscillate along a single fixed direction, instead of every direction perpendicular to travel.

**Question 2 (Malus's law, worked through):** Unpolarised light of intensity $I_0$ first passes through a polariser (intensity becomes $I_0/2$, standard for unpolarised light through any single polariser), then through a second polariser whose axis is $30^\circ$ from the first. Find the final intensity.

*Answer:* Malus's law on the second polariser: $I = \dfrac{I_0}{2}\cos^2(30^\circ) = \dfrac{I_0}{2}\times\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{3I_0}{8}$.

**Why this drill exists:** "the electric field of an electromagnetic wave" sounds like an abstract fact from the previous concept, easy to forget once interference and diffraction take over. Polarisation is that exact same electric field, restricted to one direction — the two concepts are describing the same physical quantity from two different angles.
