**Problem:** 2-bromo-3-methylbutane, $\text{CH}_3\text{-CHBr-CH(CH}_3\text{)-CH}_3$, is treated under conditions that let the carbon-bromine bond break on its own (ionisation, without a nucleophile pushing from behind). The bromine-bearing carbon is secondary. Does the reaction stop at the secondary carbocation, or does something else happen first?

---

**Step 1 — Form the first carbocation.** The $\text{C-Br}$ bond breaks, bromide ion leaves carrying the bonding electron pair with it, and the carbon that held it (carbon 2) becomes a carbocation: three bonds, an empty p-orbital, a positive charge. It is joined to two other carbons — secondary.

---

**Step 2 — Look one carbon over, before attacking.** Carbon 3, right next to the new cation, is joined to three other carbons (the main chain on both sides, plus a branch methyl) and one hydrogen — a tertiary position.

---

**Step 3 — Name the two competing outcomes.** Either the nucleophile attacks the secondary carbocation exactly where it formed, or the molecule rearranges first and the nucleophile attacks a more stable carbocation instead. Both are physically possible; only one is favoured.

---

**Step 4 — Decide which one wins, by comparing stabilities.** A tertiary carbocation sits lower in energy than a secondary one — more neighbouring carbons mean more hyperconjugating $\text{C-H}$ bonds and more inductive donation into the empty orbital. Since carbon 3 can become tertiary by giving up just one hydrogen, the molecule takes that route: the electron pair of the $\text{C-H}$ bond on carbon 3 shifts across to fill carbon 2's empty orbital, and the positive charge moves to carbon 3.

---

**Step 5 — State the outcome.** $\boxed{\text{the more stable tertiary carbocation forms; the nucleophile ends up on carbon 3, not carbon 2}}$ — a hydride shift, not an error in the mechanism, because the new position is genuinely more comfortable for the missing electron pair.
