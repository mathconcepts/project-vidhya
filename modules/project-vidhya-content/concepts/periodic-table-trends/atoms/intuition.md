The single idea behind every periodic trend across a row is effective nuclear charge: the pull an outer electron actually feels, after other electrons in the same shell partly block (or shield) the nucleus's full pull. Moving left to right across a period, protons increase by one each step but the new electron goes into the same shell, adding almost no extra shielding, so effective nuclear charge rises steadily and pulls each outer electron in tighter. That single fact explains three trends at once: atomic radius shrinks, ionisation enthalpy rises (electrons are held harder, so removing one costs more energy), and electronegativity rises (the atom pulls shared electrons harder too).

But rises steadily is not the same as rises at every single step, and two structural exceptions sit exactly here. An electron sitting alone in an orbital is more stable than one forced to share an orbital with another electron, because two electrons in one orbital repel each other extra (electron-pair repulsion). A filled subshell like $2s^2$, or a half-filled subshell like $2p^3$ (one electron in each of three 2p orbitals), is unusually stable for this reason. So going from beryllium ($2s^2$, filled) to boron ($2s^22p^1$, its newest electron forced into a higher-energy orbital), that new electron is actually easier to remove, not harder, and enthalpy dips. The same thing happens from nitrogen ($2p^3$, half-filled, no repulsion) to oxygen ($2p^4$, one orbital now holds a repelling pair) — the paired electron is easier to remove than nitrogen's lone ones were.

Down a group, the story flips: each step adds a whole new outer shell, sitting far from the nucleus and shielded by every inner shell. Atomic radius grows, and even with more protons pulling, the extra distance and shielding win — so ionisation enthalpy and electronegativity both fall down a group, reliably, because a brand-new shell is a much bigger structural change than one more electron inside the same shell.
