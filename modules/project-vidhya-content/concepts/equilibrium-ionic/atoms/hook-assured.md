A buffer "holding the pH steady" is often read as "stopping the pH from changing at all." It does neither — it slows the change, and only within a range.

Henderson–Hasselbalch, $\text{pH}=\text{p}K_a+\log_{10}([\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}])$, shows why: the pH tracks the *ratio* of base to acid, not the absolute amount of either. Add strong acid, and some $\text{A}^-$ converts to $\text{HA}$ — the ratio shifts a little, so pH shifts a little too, just far less than in unbuffered water.

Push past that and the buffer fails: exhaust the conjugate base entirely, and there is nothing left to soak up the next drop of acid. The equation itself hints at the limit — it silently assumes both $[\text{HA}]$ and $[\text{A}^-]$ stay comfortably above zero, a condition every real buffer eventually stops meeting.
