A **mole** is just a counting word, like "a dozen" or "a gross" — except the number it stands for is enormous: $6.022 \times 10^{23}$, called **Avogadro's number** ($N_A$). One mole of anything — atoms, molecules, ions — is that many of them.

Chemists never weigh out one atom at a time; atoms are far too light and far too numerous for that. Instead they weigh out grams, then convert to moles using the **molar mass** (the mass, in grams, of one mole of a substance — numerically equal to the atomic or molecular mass in amu). $\text{moles} = \dfrac{\text{mass}}{\text{molar mass}}$.

Once a quantity is in moles, a balanced chemical equation's coefficients directly say how many moles of each substance react or form — the whole point of converting to moles in the first place. The two everyday numbers that describe a substance — how much it weighs, and how many particles that is — are connected by exactly one bridge: the molar mass.
