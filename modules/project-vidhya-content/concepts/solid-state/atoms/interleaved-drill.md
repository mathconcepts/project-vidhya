**Cross-concept check: solid-state → chemical-bonding.**

**Question 1 (bond type decides the lattice):** Sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) is held together by ionic bonding, while diamond is held together by a continuous network of covalent bonds. Which of these two is more likely to be extremely hard and have an extremely high melting point, and why?

*Answer:* Diamond. A covalent NETWORK solid has every atom bonded to its neighbours through strong, directional covalent bonds extending through the entire crystal — breaking it apart means breaking actual covalent bonds throughout. An ionic solid like $\text{NaCl}$ is held by electrostatic attraction between ions, strong but non-directional, and though $\text{NaCl}$'s melting point is high too, it is nowhere near diamond's.

**Question 2 (bond type decides the packing you'd expect):** Why do simple, roughly-spherical metal atoms (bonded by metallic bonding, which is non-directional) typically crystallise in the HIGH packing-efficiency structures (bcc or fcc), while covalently-bonded diamond does not?

*Answer:* Metallic bonding pulls equally in every direction, so metal atoms behave like featureless spheres free to pack as tightly as geometry allows — exactly the bcc/fcc structures this concept describes. Diamond's covalent bonds are strongly DIRECTIONAL (each carbon bonds to exactly $4$ neighbours at fixed tetrahedral angles), so its atoms cannot rearrange into a close-packed structure without breaking that fixed bonding geometry — diamond's own packing efficiency is far below fcc's $74\%$, because directional bonding, not geometry alone, decides its structure.

**Why this drill exists:** a crystal's structure is not a free geometric choice — it is a direct consequence of the type of chemical bond holding it together, the exact idea chemical bonding already covers. Packing efficiency numbers only make sense for bonding that lets atoms behave like simple, non-directional spheres.
