You already know why amines are basic: nitrogen's lone pair of electrons can accept a proton ($\text{H}^+$). The restriction the school course usually glosses over: "more alkyl groups attached to nitrogen means more basic" is true in the gas phase, but water flips part of that order.

In the **gas phase** (no water molecules around to interfere), only one effect matters — how much electron density the alkyl groups push onto nitrogen. Each alkyl group is electron-donating, so more alkyl groups means a nitrogen lone pair that is more available to grab a proton. The order there is exactly what you'd expect: tertiary amine is the most basic, then secondary, then primary, with plain ammonia the weakest.

In **water**, a second effect enters the picture: once the amine grabs a proton, the resulting ammonium ion needs to be stabilised by hydrogen bonds to surrounding water molecules — and it can only form those bonds through the $\text{N-H}$ bonds it has left. A primary ammonium ion ($\text{RNH}_3^+$) has three $\text{N-H}$ bonds available for hydrogen bonding; a tertiary ammonium ion ($\text{R}_3\text{NH}^+$) has only one. Fewer $\text{N-H}$ bonds means weaker stabilisation by water, which pulls the tertiary amine's basicity back down — enough that, for simple methyl-substituted amines, the observed aqueous order becomes secondary > primary > tertiary > ammonia. The electron-donation effect never goes away; solvation just fights back hardest against the amine with the fewest $\text{N-H}$ bonds to hydrogen-bond with.
