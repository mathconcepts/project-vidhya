The force on a moving charge, $\vec F=q\vec v\times\vec B$, is a **cross product** — and a cross product cares about direction far more than it cares about size. Its magnitude is $F=qvB\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle *between* $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. That $\sin\theta$ factor is the whole story: at $\theta=90°$ (velocity perpendicular to field), $\sin\theta=1$ and the force is at its largest. At $\theta=0°$ or $180°$ (velocity parallel or antiparallel to the field), $\sin\theta=0$ and the force vanishes completely — no matter how fast the charge moves or how strong the field is.

To find the *direction* of the force on a **positive** charge, curl the fingers of your right hand from $\vec v$ towards $\vec B$ (through the smaller angle) — the thumb points along $\vec F$. For a **negative** charge, the force points exactly opposite to what the right hand gives — flip it.

A separate, related rule applies to a current-carrying wire rather than a single moving charge: $\vec F=I\vec L\times\vec B$, where $\vec L$ points along the current's direction. Indian classrooms usually pair this one with **Fleming's Left-Hand Rule** (thumb = force, first finger = field, middle finger = current) — a different hand from the right-hand rule used for a single moving charge, and mixing the two up is an easy, common slip.
