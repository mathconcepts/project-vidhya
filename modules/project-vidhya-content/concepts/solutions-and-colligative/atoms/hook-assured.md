$i=2$ for $\text{NaCl}$ assumes complete dissociation — true for a strong electrolyte in dilute solution, but nowhere near a general rule for "any ionic compound."

A WEAK electrolyte only partly dissociates: $i=1+(n-1)\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the actual fraction that splits apart and $n$ is the number of particles complete dissociation would give. An acid that is only $2\%$ dissociated ($\alpha=0.02$, $n=2$) gives $i=1.02$ — nowhere near the full $i=2$ a strong electrolyte's formula might suggest.

Solutes can even ASSOCIATE instead of dissociate — benzoic acid dimerises in benzene, giving TWO molecules acting as ONE particle, so $i<1$ there. **Counting the formula's ions is necessary information, but nowhere near sufficient** — the actual particle count in solution, which can be less than, equal to, or more than the formula suggests, is what a colligative-property formula actually needs.
