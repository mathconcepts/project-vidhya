You already know polymers are long chains built from small repeating units, called monomers. The one question that sorts every polymer into one of two families is: does forming the chain release a small molecule at each join, or not?

**Addition polymerisation** answers no. The monomer already has a double bond, and that double bond simply opens up so one monomer links directly to the next — nothing is lost along the way. Polythene, from ethylene ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2$) monomers, is the standard example: every atom in the monomer ends up somewhere in the final chain.

**Condensation polymerisation** answers yes. Two different monomers, each carrying a reactive end group, join at each step with the loss of a small molecule — usually water. Nylon-6,6 forms this way: a diamine and a diacid react, and one water molecule leaves at every single bond formed along the growing chain.

Reading a monomer's structure and asking "is there a double bond to open, or a small molecule to lose" tells you which family a polymer belongs to before anything else about it needs to be known.
