**Cross-concept check: kinematics-1d → kinematics-2d.**

**Question 1 (the vertical part of a 2D launch is just 1D motion):** A projectile is launched with vertical velocity component $u_y=12$ m/s (upward positive), $g=10$ m/s$^2$. Find its vertical velocity and vertical position at $t=0.6$ s.

*Answer:* This is a plain 1D motion problem in the vertical direction alone — the horizontal motion does not enter at all. $v_y = u_y - gt = 12-10(0.6)=6$ m/s (still moving upward). $y=u_yt-\tfrac{1}{2}gt^2 = 12(0.6)-5(0.6)^2=7.2-1.8=5.4$ m.

**Question 2 (what stays the same, what changes, going to two dimensions):** In the 2D version of this launch, does the horizontal velocity affect either of these two vertical answers?

*Answer:* No. Once the launch velocity is split into $u_x$ and $u_y$ components using the sign convention set up for each axis, the vertical motion runs on its own 1D equations exactly as above, completely independent of whatever the horizontal velocity is doing. This split is the entire trick behind two-dimensional motion under gravity.

**Why this drill exists:** every projectile-motion problem is secretly two separate 1D kinematics problems — one along each axis — solved side by side and then combined only at the end. Nothing new needs to be learned about the vertical direction; it is this exact concept, applied a second time.
