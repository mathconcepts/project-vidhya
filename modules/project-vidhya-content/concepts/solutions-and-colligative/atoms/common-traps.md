- **Assuming colligative properties depend on WHAT the solute is, not just how many particles it gives**: sugar and salt at the same molal concentration do NOT give the same freezing-point depression — because salt splits into ions and sugar does not, giving a different particle count, never because the two substances are "different chemicals" in some other sense.

- **Confusing molality with molarity**: $\Delta T_b=iK_bm$ and $\Delta T_f=iK_fm$ both need MOLALITY (moles of solute per KILOGRAM of solvent), while osmotic pressure needs MOLARITY (moles of solute per LITRE of solution). Using molarity where molality belongs (or the reverse) silently gives a wrong answer whenever the solution's density is not close to $1\ \text{kg/L}$.

- **Assuming the van't Hoff factor is always a whole number**: $i$ is a whole number ONLY for a fully-dissociating strong electrolyte. A weak electrolyte gives a fractional $i$ between $1$ and $n$ (like $i=1.02$ for a barely-ionising acid); an associating solute can even give $i<1$.

- **Treating relative lowering of vapour pressure and molality-based formulas as using the same concentration term**: relative lowering of vapour pressure uses MOLE FRACTION, not molality — a subtle but genuine source of error, since mole fraction and molality only track each other closely in very dilute solutions.

- **Forgetting $i$ entirely when finding molar mass from colligative data**: skipping $i$ (implicitly assuming $i=1$) for a solute that actually ionises or associates gives an "apparent" molar mass that is systematically too low (for an ionising solute) or too high (for an associating one) — not a rounding error, but a genuine, avoidable mistake.
