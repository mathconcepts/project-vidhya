Picture eight steps of a staircase, one for each element of Period 2 from lithium to neon, each step's height set by that element's real first ionisation enthalpy (in kJ/mol). A perfectly built staircase would have every step higher than the one before it — that is what "rises across a period" would look like if the rule held everywhere.

The bars on this card are that real staircase, and it is not perfectly built. Two steps are shorter than the one just before them: boron's bar dips below beryllium's, and oxygen's bar dips below nitrogen's. Everywhere else, each bar is taller than its neighbour to the left, exactly as the general trend predicts. The two dips are not random unevenness in the data — they sit at exactly the two places where the outermost electron moves from a specially stable subshell arrangement (filled $2s^2$, or half-filled $2p^3$) into a less stable one (a lone $2p^1$ electron, or a forced-to-pair $2p^4$ electron). Seeing the dip is seeing the exception; naming which subshell change caused it is what a JEE question is really asking for.
