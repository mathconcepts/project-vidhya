- **Treating "ionisation enthalpy always rises across a period" as absolute**: this is the trend you memorised, and the exam is built on exactly the two points where it does not hold. $IE(Be) = 899 > IE(B) = 801$ (a filled $2s^2$ subshell is harder to break into than a lone electron in $2p$), and $IE(N) = 1402 > IE(O) = 1314$ (half-filled $2p^3$, no pairing, beats a $2p^4$ with a repelling pair). Any "arrange these elements by ionisation enthalpy" question with Be/B or N/O in the list is almost always testing whether you know these two flips, not whether you know the general trend.

- **Assuming smaller atom always means more negative electron gain enthalpy**: fluorine is smaller than chlorine, so it should attract an added electron more strongly — but fluorine's electron gain enthalpy ($-328$ kJ/mol) is *less* negative than chlorine's ($-349$ kJ/mol). Fluorine's very small $2p$ subshell is already electron-dense, so the incoming electron faces extra repulsion that chlorine's larger, less crowded $3p$ subshell does not.

- **Ignoring nuclear charge for isoelectronic ions**: $Na^+$, $Mg^{2+}$ and $Al^{3+}$ all have exactly 10 electrons (same as neon), so it is tempting to think they are the same size. They are not — radius shrinks from $Na^+$ to $Al^{3+}$, because the same 10 electrons are pulled in by 11, then 12, then 13 protons. Same electron count does not mean same radius; nuclear charge still decides it.

- **Assuming the diagonal relationship is just a curiosity, never tested numerically**: lithium behaves more like magnesium than like sodium for several real properties — both $LiOH$ and $Mg(OH)_2$ are comparatively weak, sparingly soluble bases, unlike the strongly soluble $NaOH$. A question describing lithium's chemistry as "unusually similar to a Group 2 element" is naming the diagonal relationship, not making an error.

- **Forgetting that only two anomaly points exist per period, not one everywhere**: after learning about the Be/B and N/O dips, some students expect a similar dip between O and F, or between C and N. There is none — those steps are ordinary additions to an already-progressing subshell, with no new filled-or-half-filled state being broken.
