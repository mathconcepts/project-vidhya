**Carbonyl group**: $\text{C=O}$. Oxygen's higher electronegativity (its stronger pull on shared electrons) leaves the carbon short of electron density — this carbon is electrophilic (electron-seeking) and is where nucleophiles attack.

**Alpha-hydrogen**: a hydrogen on the carbon directly bonded to the carbonyl carbon. Its presence or absence is the single fork that decides aldol versus Cannizzaro below.

**Nucleophilic addition**: the carbonyl carbon's pi bond breaks as a nucleophile bonds to it; the displaced pi electrons move fully onto the oxygen, which is then protonated. This is the base reaction every carbonyl transformation in this concept builds on.

**Aldol condensation**: for a carbonyl compound WITH an alpha-hydrogen, dilute base removes it to form an enolate, which attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule of the same compound, giving a beta-hydroxy aldehyde or ketone that can lose water on heating to give an alpha,beta-unsaturated carbonyl compound.

**Cannizzaro reaction**: for a carbonyl compound WITHOUT an alpha-hydrogen, concentrated base attacks the carbonyl carbon directly; the resulting intermediate transfers a hydride ion to a second molecule. One molecule is oxidised to the carboxylate salt, the other reduced to the alcohol.

**Reactivity order (nucleophilic addition)**: $\text{HCHO} > \text{RCHO} > \text{R}_2\text{CO}$, i.e. aldehydes react faster than ketones. Fewer and smaller groups on the carbonyl carbon mean less steric crowding of the attacking nucleophile and less electron-donation cancelling the carbon's electrophilicity.

**Distinguishing an aldehyde from a ketone**: Tollens' reagent ($\text{Ag(NH}_3)_2^+$) oxidises an aldehyde to the carboxylate and is itself reduced to metallic silver (the silver-mirror test); ketones do not reduce it. Fehling's solution behaves the same way for aliphatic aldehydes.

**Acidity of carboxylic acids**: $\text{R-COOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{R-COO}^- + \text{H}^+$. The carboxylate ion is stabilised because its negative charge is shared equally between both oxygens (resonance), which is not available to an alcohol's conjugate base — this is why carboxylic acids are far more acidic than alcohols with the same $\text{R}$ group. An electron-withdrawing group on $\text{R}$ pulls electron density away from the carboxylate, spreading its negative charge further and increasing acidity; an electron-donating group does the opposite.
