Think of $\int f(x)\,dx$ as reconstructing a bank balance from a record of daily transactions, $f(x)$. Knowing every deposit and withdrawal tells you exactly how the balance CHANGED — but not the balance itself, because nobody wrote down the opening amount. Two people looking at the same transaction record could have started with ₹0 or ₹5,000; both curves rise and fall in lockstep, offset by a fixed amount forever. That fixed, unrecoverable offset is exactly the $+C$ in an indefinite integral: the family of all antiderivatives of $f$ is one shape, shifted up or down by every possible constant.

This is also why an indefinite integral is never "the" antiderivative, only "an" antiderivative up to $+C$ — and why a definite integral (a difference of two balances) makes $C$ vanish: whatever opening balance you guessed, it cancels when you subtract one point on the curve from another.
