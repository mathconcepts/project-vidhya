Stretch a rubber band and let go — it snaps back to its original shape almost every time. Stretch a plastic carry bag the same way and it stays stretched, permanently deformed. Both are made of long, repeating chains of molecules called **polymers** (from small repeating units, or monomers, joined together many times over), so why does one spring back and the other doesn't?

Natural rubber's long chains are treated with sulfur in a process called **vulcanisation**, which creates cross-links — extra bonds joining one chain to its neighbours at scattered points along their length, like rungs holding two ropes together. Those cross-links let the whole tangled network stretch out and then snap back, because no single chain can slide permanently past its neighbours. An ordinary plastic bag's chains have no such cross-links: stretch them and they simply slide past each other and stay in their new, stretched positions. One structural feature — cross-linking, present or absent — decides whether a polymer remembers its shape or not.
