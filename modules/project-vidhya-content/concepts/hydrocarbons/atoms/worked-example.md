**Problem:** Cyclopentadiene is a five-membered ring with two double bonds and one $\text{CH}_2$ group that is not part of either double bond. Removing a proton from that $\text{CH}_2$ gives the **cyclopentadienyl anion**, $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5^-$. Removing a hydride ion from the same spot instead would give the **cyclopentadienyl cation**, $\text{C}_5\text{H}_5^+$. Which of these two ions is actually the aromatic one?

---

**Step 1 — Notice why this is a genuine choice, not an obvious one.** Both ions have the same five-carbon ring, fully flat, and both come from the same starting molecule by removing something from the same carbon. A quick guess — "a positive charge on a ring sounds like the more electron-hungry, more reactive-seeming case" — is not evidence either way. Only counting electrons settles it.

---

**Step 2 — Count pi electrons in the anion.** The two original double bonds contribute $4$ electrons. Removing a proton (not a hydride) leaves the lone pair that carbon used to share with that proton sitting in a p-orbital of its own, contributing $2$ more electrons to the ring. Total: $4+2=6$ pi electrons.

---

**Step 3 — Count pi electrons in the cation.** The two original double bonds still contribute $4$ electrons. Removing a hydride ion takes both electrons of that $\text{C-H}$ bond away with it, leaving an *empty* p-orbital contributing $0$ electrons. Total: $4+0=4$ pi electrons.

---

**Step 4 — Apply Huckel's rule to each count.** For the anion: $4n+2=6$ gives $n=1$, a whole number — aromatic. For the cation: $4n+2=4$ gives $n=0.5$, not a whole number, and $4$ is exactly a $4n$ count — antiaromatic, not aromatic.

---

**Step 5 — State the answer.** $\boxed{\text{the cyclopentadienyl anion, } \text{C}_5\text{H}_5^-\text{, is the aromatic one; the cation is antiaromatic}}$ — the opposite of what "a stabilised positive ring" might suggest, because aromaticity depends entirely on the electron count, not on the sign of the charge.
