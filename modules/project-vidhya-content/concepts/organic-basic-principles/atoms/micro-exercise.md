Rank these four carbocations from **least** stable to **most** stable: $\text{CH}_3^+$ (methyl), $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2^+$ (ethyl, primary), $(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH}^+$ (isopropyl, secondary), $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+$ (tert-butyl, tertiary).

A) tert-butyl $<$ isopropyl $<$ ethyl $<$ methyl
B) methyl $<$ ethyl $<$ isopropyl $<$ tert-butyl
C) ethyl $<$ methyl $<$ isopropyl $<$ tert-butyl
D) All four are equally stable since they are all carbocations
E) methyl $<$ isopropyl $<$ ethyl $<$ tert-butyl

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct answer: B) methyl $<$ ethyl $<$ isopropyl $<$ tert-butyl.**

Count the neighbouring carbons on the positive carbon in each case: methyl has zero, ethyl has one, isopropyl has two, tert-butyl has three. Each extra neighbour adds more $\text{C-H}$ bonds that can donate sideways into the empty orbital (hyperconjugation) and more inductive push from the extra carbons — so stability rises in exactly the same order as the neighbour count.

**Why the others fail:**
- A) reverses the whole order.
- C) swaps methyl and ethyl — methyl has *zero* stabilising neighbours, so it must be the least stable, not second-least.
- D) is wrong: a positive charge sitting on a carbon with no help nearby is far higher in energy than one with three donating neighbours.
- E) swaps isopropyl and ethyl — isopropyl has two donating neighbours to ethyl's one, so it must rank higher.

</details>
