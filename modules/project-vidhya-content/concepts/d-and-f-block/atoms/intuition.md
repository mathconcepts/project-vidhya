Three facts that look separate in a textbook — colour, magnetism, and having more than one common oxidation state — all trace back to one thing: how many electrons sit, unpaired, in a partly filled $d$ subshell.

Colour needs empty $d$ orbitals close in energy to filled ones, so an electron can absorb a photon of visible light and jump between them; a completely empty ($d^0$) or completely full ($d^{10}$) subshell has nowhere for that jump to land, so it shows no colour. Magnetism needs unpaired electrons, since each unpaired electron behaves like a tiny magnet that lines up with an external field (called paramagnetism); a filled or empty subshell has every electron paired off, cancelling out, so it is unaffected by a magnet (diamagnetic). Variable oxidation states need $d$ electrons that are close enough in energy to the outer $s$ electrons to join or leave bonding depending on what a reaction demands — so an element can lose just its $s$ electrons for a low oxidation state, or several $d$ electrons on top for a higher one, because none of those energies are far enough apart to lock the element into one fixed number.

All three properties, in other words, are just different consequences of the same fact: a $d$ subshell that is neither empty nor full has electrons available to absorb light, to respond to a magnet, and to be pulled into or held back from bonding, all through the same partly filled subshell.
