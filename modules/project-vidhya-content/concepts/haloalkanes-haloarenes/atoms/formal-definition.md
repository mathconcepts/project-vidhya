**$S_N2$**: one step. The nucleophile attacks from directly opposite the leaving group as it departs. Rate depends on *both* substrate and nucleophile concentration: $\text{rate} = k[\text{substrate}][\text{nucleophile}]$. Gives **inversion of configuration**. Favoured by an unhindered (primary) carbon, a strong nucleophile, and a polar **aprotic** solvent (one with no $\text{O-H}$ or $\text{N-H}$ to trap the nucleophile).

**$S_N1$**: two steps. The leaving group departs first, forming a carbocation; the nucleophile attacks afterwards, from either face. Rate depends only on substrate concentration: $\text{rate} = k[\text{substrate}]$. Gives **racemisation** (roughly equal parts of both spatial arrangements). Favoured by a carbon whose resulting carbocation is stable (tertiary, or benzylic/allylic), a weak nucleophile, and a polar **protic** solvent (one that stabilises the developing charges by hydrogen bonding).

**$E2$**: one step. A base removes a hydrogen from a carbon next to the one bearing the leaving group, while that leaving group departs at the same time, forming a double bond. Favoured by a strong, often bulky base. Usually gives the more substituted alkene (**Zaitsev's product**) as the major one, unless the base is especially bulky, in which case the less hindered, less substituted alkene (the Hofmann product) becomes major instead.

**$E1$**: two steps, sharing the same carbocation intermediate as $S_N1$ — the leaving group departs first, then a base removes a neighbouring hydrogen. Competes with $S_N1$ under the same conditions (tertiary substrate, weak base/nucleophile).

**Substrate reactivity trend**: $S_N2$ rate falls as the carbon gets more crowded — primary $>$ secondary $>$ tertiary (tertiary is essentially unreactive by this route). $S_N1$ rate rises with carbocation stability — tertiary $>$ secondary $>$ primary (primary is essentially unreactive by this route).

**Low reactivity of aryl halides**: (1) the halogen's lone pair delocalises into the ring by resonance, giving the carbon-halogen bond partial double-bond character — shorter and stronger than an ordinary single bond; (2) the ring carbon is $sp^2$, which holds its bonding electrons more tightly than an $sp^3$ carbon does, further strengthening the bond; (3) an aryl carbocation, needed for $S_N1$, would have its empty orbital lying in the ring's own plane, unable to receive any resonance stabilisation from the ring's pi system, so it essentially never forms; (4) the ring itself physically blocks backside attack, ruling out $S_N2$ as well.

**Grignard reagents** ($\text{RMgX}$): made by reacting an alkyl or aryl halide with magnesium metal in dry ether. The carbon attached to magnesium behaves as a carbanion-equivalent (an electron-rich, nucleophilic carbon), reacting readily with carbonyl compounds to form alcohols. Grignard reagents are destroyed instantly by even trace moisture or by any acidic hydrogen (from $-\text{OH}$, $-\text{NH}$, or $-\text{COOH}$ groups), which is why they must be prepared and used under completely dry conditions.
