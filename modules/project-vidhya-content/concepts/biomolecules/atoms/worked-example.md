**Problem:** A sugar solution is warmed with Fehling's reagent (a blue $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ solution) and a brick-red precipitate forms. Is the sugar more likely glucose or sucrose?

---

**Step 1 — Recall what Fehling's test actually needs.** A brick-red precipitate of $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$ forms only when the sugar has a free anomeric carbon — one that can open into a chain form carrying a free aldehyde group able to reduce $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ to $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$.

---

**Step 2 — Check glucose against this requirement.** Glucose is a monosaccharide with one anomeric carbon, free and unattached to any other sugar unit. It can open into its chain form and expose that aldehyde group — glucose is a **reducing sugar**, and gives a positive Fehling's test.

---

**Step 3 — Check sucrose against the same requirement, as the competing candidate.** Sucrose is a disaccharide of glucose and fructose, but its glycosidic bond forms specifically between the anomeric carbons of BOTH units. Neither anomeric carbon is left free — there is no aldehyde group available anywhere in the molecule. Sucrose is a **non-reducing sugar**, and gives no reaction with Fehling's reagent at all.

$$\boxed{\text{The solution is glucose, not sucrose.}}$$

**Why sucrose is ruled out, not just glucose confirmed.** A student might reasonably think "it's a sugar, and sugars react with Fehling's" — but that reasoning would wrongly predict a positive test for sucrose too. The correct check is narrower: does this specific molecule have a free anomeric carbon left over after all its bonds are accounted for? Sucrose's glycosidic bond specifically ties up both anomeric carbons at once, which is exactly what makes it the one common disaccharide that fails this test.
