One structural fact decides a real chemistry question across the p-block: which oxidation state does an element actually prefer, the full group oxidation state (using every valence electron) or the group oxidation state minus two (leaving the outermost $s$-electron pair untouched)? The answer is called the inert pair effect. Going down a group, that outer $s$-pair is shielded from the nucleus more and more poorly by the $d$ and $f$ electrons that appear in between (from period 4 onward), and the atom itself grows large enough that its valence orbitals overlap weakly with a bonding partner anyway. Both effects make it energetically easier to just leave the $s$-pair alone, un-bonded, rather than spend extra energy promoting and pairing it into new bonds.

For the lighter members of groups 13–15 (boron, aluminium; nitrogen, phosphorus), this effect barely matters — the full group oxidation state ($+3$, $+5$) is essentially the only one seen. Moving down each group, the (group minus two) oxidation state becomes steadily more competitive, until for the heaviest common member it can overtake the group state entirely.

Group 13 shows the sharpest version of this flip: thallium's $Tl^+$ compounds are more stable than its $Tl^{3+}$ compounds — the exact reverse of boron and aluminium, where the $+3$ state completely dominates. The reversal is not a quiet, invisible shift; it produces a genuinely odd compound. Solid "thallium(III) iodide" is not $Tl^{3+}$ paired with three $I^-$ ions at all — it is $Tl^+$ paired with a triiodide ion, $I_3^-$, because $Tl^{3+}$ is too strong an oxidiser to sit next to iodide, while $Tl^+$ is perfectly happy there.
