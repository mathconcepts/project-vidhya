- **Forgetting the $\sin\theta$ factor entirely.** $F=qvB\sin\theta$ is not $F=qvB$. A charge moving parallel (or antiparallel) to the field, $\theta=0°$ or $180°$, feels **zero** force, no matter how large $q$, $v$, or $B$ are — the direction between velocity and field decides everything, not just the magnitudes.

- **Forgetting to flip the right-hand-rule result for a negative charge.** The right-hand rule (curl fingers from $\vec v$ towards $\vec B$, thumb gives $\vec F$) gives the force direction for a **positive** charge only. For a negative charge, the actual force is exactly opposite to what the right hand shows — skipping this flip is one of the most common direction errors in this topic.

- **Mixing up which "hand" applies to which situation.** The right-hand rule is for a single moving charge, $\vec F=q\vec v\times\vec B$. Fleming's Left-Hand Rule is for a current-carrying wire in a field, $\vec F=I\vec L\times\vec B$ — a genuinely different physical setup. Using the wrong hand's convention for the wrong situation gives a direction that's often backwards.

- **Reading torque's $\theta$ as the angle of the loop's plane.** In $\tau=NIAB\sin\theta$, $\theta$ is measured from the loop's **normal**, not from the plane of the loop itself. A loop lying with its plane parallel to $\vec B$ has its normal perpendicular to $\vec B$ — that's $\theta=90°$, maximum torque, the opposite of what "parallel" might suggest at a glance.
