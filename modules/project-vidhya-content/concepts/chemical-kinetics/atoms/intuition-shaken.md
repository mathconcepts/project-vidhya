A first-order reaction has rate constant $k=0.0231\ \text{min}^{-1}$.

Its half-life is $t_{1/2}=\dfrac{0.693}{k}=\dfrac{0.693}{0.0231}=30\ \text{min}$.

Starting at $[A]_0=1.6\ \text{mol/L}$: after $30\ \text{min}$, $[A]=0.8\ \text{mol/L}$ (halved once). After another $30\ \text{min}$ ($60\ \text{min}$ total), $[A]=0.4\ \text{mol/L}$ (halved again). After a third $30\ \text{min}$ ($90\ \text{min}$ total), $[A]=0.2\ \text{mol/L}$.

Check with the exponential form directly: $[A]=1.6\,e^{-0.0231\times90}=1.6\,e^{-2.079}\approx1.6\times0.125=0.2\ \text{mol/L}$ — matches. The half-life stayed exactly $30\ \text{min}$ at every stage, whatever the concentration happened to be — this constant halving time is unique to first-order kinetics.
