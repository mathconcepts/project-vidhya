The water-versus-gas-phase basicity flip (secondary > primary > tertiary in water, reversed in the gas phase) is familiar ground. It applies only when comparing amines of the SAME broad type — swapping in an aromatic amine breaks it completely, for a reason neither solvation nor simple electron-donation covers.

Counterexample: aniline ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2$) has one alkyl-like substituent (a phenyl ring) attached to nitrogen, the same substitution count as methylamine. Ranking by "more substituents pushed onto nitrogen, more basic" would place it near a primary alkyl amine. In reality aniline is markedly LESS basic than ammonia itself. The ring is not donating electron density the way an alkyl group does — nitrogen's lone pair instead delocalises INTO the aromatic ring by resonance, becoming partly shared with the ring's pi system and far less available to grab a proton. This resonance effect has nothing to do with solvation or induction and simply dominates them; treating every "amine with a substituent on nitrogen" the same way, aliphatic or aromatic, is exactly where this topic costs marks.
