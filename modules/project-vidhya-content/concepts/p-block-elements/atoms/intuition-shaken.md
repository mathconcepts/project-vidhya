Two real facts about tin and lead, both Group 14: $SnCl_4$ (tin, oxidation state $+4$) is a common, stable, colourless liquid. $PbO_2$ (lead, oxidation state $+4$) is not stable in the same easy way — it is actually a strong oxidising agent, meaning it "wants" to grab electrons and drop back down to $Pb^{2+}$. Same group, same column, one step down, and the higher oxidation state flips from easy to unstable.

Why? Each atom's outermost electron pair sits in an $s$-orbital. In tin, that pair is reasonably close to the nucleus and gets involved in bonding without much fuss. In lead, one period further down, that same $s$-pair is shielded from the nucleus by a whole extra layer of $d$ and $f$ electrons sitting in between, and lead's orbitals are large enough to overlap only weakly with a bonding partner. So it costs more energy to drag lead's $s$-pair into bonds than it does for tin's — lead would rather leave that pair alone, giving $Pb^{2+}$, not $Pb^{4+}$. This effect, the reluctance of the outer $s$-pair to bond as you go down a group, is called the inert pair effect.

Group 13 shows the same shift taken all the way: aluminium's $+3$ state is completely normal, but thallium's $+1$ state ($Tl^+$) is actually more stable than $Tl^{3+}$. Check it against a real compound: solid "thallium(III) iodide" turns out not to be $Tl^{3+}$ with three $I^-$ ions — it is $Tl^+$ paired with $I_3^-$, because $Tl^{3+}$ oxidises iodide before the compound can even form that way.
