Take one real ion, two real ligands: $Co^{3+}$, configuration $3d^6$. With $NH_3$: $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$ is diamagnetic. With $F^-$: $[CoF_6]^{3-}$ is paramagnetic. Same metal, same charge, same 6 electrons in $3d$ — completely different magnetism.

Check the spectrochemical series: $NH_3$ ranks as a stronger-field ligand than $F^-$ for $Co^{3+}$. That single ranking decides everything below.

In VBT's language: $NH_3$, strong field, forces all 6 of cobalt's $d$ electrons to pair up inside the $3d$ subshell, freeing two inner $3d$ orbitals for hybridisation — $d^2sp^3$, an inner orbital complex, $0$ unpaired electrons, diamagnetic. $F^-$, weak field, cannot force that pairing, so cobalt must reach for outer $4d$ orbitals instead — $sp^3d^2$, an outer orbital complex, with unpaired electrons left over, paramagnetic.

In CFT's language, the same two facts appear differently: $NH_3$ opens a large energy gap between the two sets of $d$ orbitals, so all 6 electrons settle into the lower, 3-orbital set (filled: $3 \times 2 = 6$) rather than pay the cost of climbing higher — low spin, $n=0$. $F^-$ opens only a small gap, so electrons spread out across all 5 orbitals first before any pairing — high spin, $n=4$. Same electron count, same ligand-strength fact, described in two vocabularies.
