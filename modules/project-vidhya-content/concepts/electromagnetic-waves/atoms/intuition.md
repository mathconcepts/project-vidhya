Picture the capacitor's two plates, gap between them, current-carrying wire feeding the top plate. Draw a small circular loop around that wire, close to the plate. Now imagine two different surfaces this same loop could bound: a flat disc that stays just above the top plate (the current-carrying wire pierces it), and a stretched, sock-shaped surface that dips down through the gap and comes back up outside the plates (nothing physical pierces it there — the wire never enters the gap).

Ampere's original law says the magnetic field around the loop depends only on the current through *whichever* surface you pick — but here the two surfaces disagree completely, $2\text{ A}$ versus $0\text{ A}$. That cannot be right for a genuine physical field, which does not care which imaginary surface a person happens to draw.

Maxwell's resolution: inside the sock-shaped surface, no charge moves, but the electric field between the plates is changing every instant as they charge. Counting that changing field as a "displacement current" of exactly $2\text{ A}$ restores agreement — both surfaces now report the same total current, and the magnetic field around the loop is, correctly, one single well-defined answer.
