A cell's label says $12\ \text{V}$ ($\text{V}$ for volts, the unit of electromotive force — the maximum "push" the cell can give). Connect a voltmeter straight across its terminals while a $5\ \Omega$ resistor is also drawing current from it, and the voltmeter reads only $10\ \text{V}$, not $12\ \text{V}$.

The cell isn't broken, and the label isn't wrong. The $12\ \text{V}$ is the cell's **emf** — what it would push if nothing inside it resisted the current at all. The $10\ \text{V}$ is the **terminal voltage** — what's actually available at the terminals once the cell's own **internal resistance** ($1\ \Omega$ here) takes its own small share of the push. The moment current flows, these two numbers stop being equal.
