- **Deciding the mechanism from substrate class alone, ignoring the reagent**: a tertiary substrate with a strong base present still eliminates ($E2$), even though the same substrate with a weak nucleophile in a protic solvent would give $S_N1$. The substrate only narrows which routes are possible; the reagent picks among them.

- **Treating a secondary substrate as if it always behaves one fixed way**: unlike a primary or tertiary carbon, a secondary one genuinely can go either route, and only the specific reagent and solvent decide which.

- **Expecting an aryl halide to undergo $S_N1$ or $S_N2$ under ordinary conditions**: the ring blocks backside attack, and the would-be aryl carbocation gets no resonance stabilisation because its empty orbital lies in the ring's own plane, not aligned with the ring's pi system. Aryl halides need very different, much harsher conditions to react at that carbon at all.

- **Assuming a bulky base always gives the ordinary Zaitsev (more substituted) alkene**: an especially bulky base struggles to reach a crowded hydrogen and instead removes a less hindered one, giving the less substituted Hofmann alkene as the major product.

- **Attempting to prepare a Grignard reagent from a halide that also carries an $-\text{OH}$, $-\text{NH}$, or $-\text{COOH}$ group**: the Grignard reagent, once formed, is destroyed instantly by that group's own acidic hydrogen, on the same molecule, before it can do anything useful.
