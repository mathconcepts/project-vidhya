A person's blood plasma sits at pH 7.4. Even a small shift of half a point, down to 7.0 or up to 7.8, causes real trouble in the body (the blood turning too acidic or too basic). Every day, exercise and digestion dump small amounts of acid straight into the bloodstream — yet the pH barely moves.

What holds it there is a **buffer**: a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base (the ion left behind once the acid gives up a hydrogen ion) that soaks up added acid or base before the pH can swing far. This resisting behaviour is **ionic equilibrium** at work — the acid and its ion sit in a balance, $\text{HA} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{A}^-$, that shifts just enough to absorb the disturbance, without ever running out completely.

A buffer resists a pH change. It does not stop one — add enough acid, and even blood's own buffer eventually gives way.
