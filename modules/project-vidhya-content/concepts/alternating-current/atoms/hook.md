A pure inductor — a coil with no resistance of its own — is connected straight across an AC supply whose voltage rises and falls as $V(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t)$, with $\omega = 100\text{ rad/s}$. One full cycle takes $T = 2\pi/\omega \approx 0.063\text{ s}$.

The voltage across the coil reaches its peak a quarter-cycle in, at $t \approx 0.0157\text{ s}$. The current flowing through that same coil does **not** peak at the same instant — it peaks a further quarter-cycle later, at $t \approx 0.0314\text{ s}$. Voltage and current are both oscillating at the exact same frequency, yet one is always a quarter-turn behind the other. That gap between "when voltage peaks" and "when current peaks" is the **phase difference**, and getting it wrong is where an alternating-current question is usually lost before any formula is even needed.
