Drawing a clean free-body diagram and applying $F_{\text{net}}=ma$ is the routine half. A sharper trap lives in static friction: $f_s\le\mu_sN$ is a *ceiling*, not a fixed value — friction supplies only as much force as is needed to prevent sliding, up to that ceiling.

A block resting on a rough floor with no applied horizontal force at all has static friction of exactly zero, even though $\mu_sN$ might be a large number — there is nothing for friction to resist yet. Only once an applied force pushes the block right up to the verge of sliding does static friction actually reach $\mu_sN$.

Counterexample worth keeping in mind: a student who writes $f=\mu_sN$ for every block "just in case it needs friction" will overstate friction's contribution on any block that is not on the verge of sliding, throwing off the net-force equation even when every other term was set up correctly.
