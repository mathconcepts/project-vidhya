- **Memorising "tertiary beats secondary beats primary" with no reason behind it**: the order comes from *counting* how many neighbouring carbons can donate electron density (via hyperconjugation and the inductive effect) into the electron-poor centre. Without that count, an unusual case — like a benzylic or allylic carbon getting extra help from resonance — will silently break a rule learned by rote.

- **Confusing chain isomerism with position isomerism**: chain isomers have a genuinely *different carbon skeleton* (straight versus branched); position isomers keep the exact same skeleton and functional group and only move *where* that group sits on it. Two molecules can look different on paper for either reason, and only checking the skeleton first tells them apart.

- **Treating every hydride (or methyl) shift as automatic**: a shift only happens when the destination carbon ends up genuinely *more* stable. A secondary-to-primary move, for instance, would make things worse and simply does not occur.

- **Mixing up the direction of the inductive and resonance effects on the same group**: $-\text{NH}_2$ and $-\text{OCH}_3$ pull electron density *away* through sigma bonds ($-I$, inductive) while pushing electron density strongly *into* a ring through resonance ($+M$). For these two groups the resonance push wins overall, making them electron-donors on balance — assuming the inductive direction always decides the outcome gets this backwards.

- **Assuming hyperconjugation needs an actual double bond nearby**: it needs an empty or partly-empty orbital next door — found on a carbocation, a free radical, or a genuine pi bond — not specifically a carbon-carbon double bond.
