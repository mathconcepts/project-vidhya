In 1909, researchers fired **alpha particles** (fast, positively-charged particles — each a helium nucleus: $2$ protons plus $2$ neutrons) at a thin gold foil only a few atoms thick. The accepted picture of the atom back then, J. J. Thomson's "plum pudding" model, smeared an atom's positive charge evenly through its whole volume, with electrons dotted inside it. That picture predicted every alpha particle should sail through with at most a tiny bend — nothing solid enough to bounce off anywhere inside such a soft, spread-out atom.

Almost all of them did sail straight through. But roughly $1$ in every $8000$ bounced back at an angle greater than $90°$ — some almost straight back the way they came. Rutherford described it as firing a heavy artillery shell at a sheet of tissue paper and having it bounce back and hit you.

A dead-on bounce like that is only possible if an atom's entire positive charge, and nearly all its mass, sit packed into a tiny, dense **nucleus** at the centre, with electrons orbiting far outside it — mostly empty space in between, which is why most alpha particles pass straight through undisturbed.
