"The arm did $72\text{ J}$ of work, so the arm's force was $48\text{ N}$" quietly assumes the force acted the whole $1.5\text{ m}$ in the direction of motion. The work-energy theorem gives you the *net* work by *all* forces, resolved along the displacement — it never tells you a single force's actual path or size unless that force is the only one acting, in a straight line.

The real subtlety hides inside **power**: $P = \vec F \cdot \vec v = Fv\cos\theta$. A force can be large and the object can be moving fast, yet power can still be zero — a satellite's gravitational pull does zero work on it every second it holds a circular orbit, because $\vec F$ and $\vec v$ sit at $90°$. Large force, large speed, zero power: $\cos 90° = 0$ overrides both.
