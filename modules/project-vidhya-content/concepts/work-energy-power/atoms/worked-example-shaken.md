**Problem:** A $2\text{ kg}$ block slides at $6\text{ m/s}$ on a rough floor ($\mu = 0.3$) and friction alone brings it to rest. Using the work-energy theorem, find the stopping distance. Take $g=10\text{ m/s}^2$.

---

**Step 0 — Set up before any formula.** Situation: a block, sliding, only friction acting horizontally. Frame: the floor; direction of motion is **positive**. Sign convention: friction opposes motion, so it is **negative** here.

---

**Step 1 — Friction force.** $f = \mu mg$. Numbers: $\mu=0.3$, $m=2$, $g=10$. So $f = 0.3 \times 2 \times 10 = 6\text{ N}$.

---

**Step 2 — Initial kinetic energy.** $KE_i = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(2)(36) = 36\text{ J}$.

---

**Step 3 — Final kinetic energy.** The block stops: $KE_f = 0\text{ J}$.

---

**Step 4 — Work-energy theorem.** $W_{net} = KE_f - KE_i = 0 - 36 = -36\text{ J}$. Only friction does work, so $W_{friction} = -36\text{ J}$.

---

**Step 5 — Isolate the distance travelled.** $-f d = -36 \Rightarrow -6d = -36 \Rightarrow d = 6\text{ m}$.

---

**Step 6 — Cross-check using kinematics.** Recompute the same distance: deceleration $a = f/m = 6/2 = 3\text{ m/s}^2$; $v^2 = u^2 - 2ad \Rightarrow 0 = 36 - 2(3)d \Rightarrow d = 6\text{ m}$. $\boxed{d=6\text{ m}}$, matching exactly.
