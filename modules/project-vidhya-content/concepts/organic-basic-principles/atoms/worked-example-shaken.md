**Problem:** 2-bromo-3-methylbutane ionises without help. The carbon that loses bromine (carbon 2) is secondary. What happens next?

---

**Step 1 — The bond breaks.** $\text{C-Br}$ splits, bromide takes the electron pair, carbon 2 is left with three bonds and an empty orbital — a secondary carbocation.

---

**Step 2 — Look at the very next carbon.** Carbon 3 is joined to three carbons already, plus one hydrogen. It is tertiary.

---

**Step 3 — Two possible next moves.** Either the nucleophile grabs carbon 2 right now, or one hydrogen moves first from carbon 3 to carbon 2.

---

**Step 4 — Compare the two carbocations.** Staying secondary: two neighbouring carbons donate. Becoming tertiary: three neighbouring carbons donate, more hyperconjugation, lower energy. Tertiary wins.

---

**Step 5 — Make the move and check it.** The $\text{C-H}$ bond's electron pair on carbon 3 slides to carbon 2, filling its empty orbital. Carbon 3 is left with three carbon bonds and the positive charge. $\boxed{\text{a hydride shift puts the charge on carbon 3, the more stable tertiary site}}$. Confirm: carbon 3 now touches carbon 2, carbon 4, and the branch methyl — three carbons, tertiary, exactly as claimed.
