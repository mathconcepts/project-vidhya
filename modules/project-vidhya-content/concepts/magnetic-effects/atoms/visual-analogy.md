Picture swimming straight along the direction a river's current flows — the water pushes you forward, but it does not shove you sideways at all, because there is no "across" component to push against. Now turn and swim straight *across* the river instead — the current now pushes you sideways with its full strength, since your motion has nothing in common with the current's own direction anymore.

A charge's encounter with a magnetic field works the same way, with the roles of "along" and "across" swapped for force: moving *along* the field direction gives **zero** magnetic force (nothing to push against), while moving *across* the field gives the **maximum** possible force for that speed and field strength. The force always builds up as the sine of the angle between the two directions, growing smoothly from zero (parallel) to a peak (perpendicular) as that angle opens up from $0°$ to $90°$.

Around a long, straight current-carrying wire, the field itself curls in circles — hold the wire with your right thumb pointing along the current, and your curled fingers trace the direction the field circles it in.
