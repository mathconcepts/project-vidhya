Think of a graph as a hiking trail seen from the side. Walking left to right: climbing means the trail's slope is positive, descending means it is negative. The very top of a hill is the one spot where you are neither climbing nor descending — for an instant, the trail is flat, slope exactly zero. That single picture is monotonicity (climbing vs. descending) and maxima (the flat instant at a peak) unified into one idea: the sign of the derivative tells you which way the trail is going, and a sign change from positive to negative marks a peak.

The tangent line is the trail's own straight-line stand-in at one exact point — the single straight path that matches the trail's direction there better than any other straight line could. Its slope is the derivative, by definition. The normal line is just that same stand-in, rotated a quarter turn — perpendicular to whichever way the trail happens to be heading at that instant, which is why its slope is the tangent's negative reciprocal, never the same number.
