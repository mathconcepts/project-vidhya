**Cross-concept check: chemical-bonding → thermodynamics-chemistry.**

**Question 1 (bond enthalpies estimate a reaction's ΔH):** Given bond enthalpies (energy to break one mole of that bond): $\text{H–H} = 436$ kJ/mol, $\text{Cl–Cl} = 242$ kJ/mol, $\text{H–Cl} = 431$ kJ/mol. Estimate $\Delta H$ for $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{HCl}$.

*Answer:* $\Delta H = (\text{bonds broken}) - (\text{bonds formed}) = (436+242) - 2(431) = 678 - 862 = -184$ kJ/mol. Breaking bonds always *costs* energy (positive); forming bonds always *releases* energy (subtracted here) — the reaction is exothermic since more energy is released forming the new bonds than was spent breaking the old ones.

**Question 2 (a stronger bond means a bigger energy cost to break, not a bigger release when it forms):** If $\text{H–Cl}$'s bond enthalpy were mistakenly used as $236$ kJ/mol (a much weaker bond) instead of $431$ kJ/mol, would the calculated $\Delta H$ become more or less negative?

*Answer:* Less negative (or possibly positive): $(436+242) - 2(236) = 678-472=206$ kJ/mol — a weaker $\text{H–Cl}$ bond releases less energy on formation, directly shrinking the exothermic result. The bond-enthalpy method's accuracy depends entirely on using the correct enthalpy value for each specific bond.

**Why this drill exists:** the same bond concept — how much energy a bond holds — feeds directly into thermodynamics' enthalpy calculations; a chemical bond is, energetically, exactly the quantity Hess's law and bond-enthalpy methods add and subtract.
