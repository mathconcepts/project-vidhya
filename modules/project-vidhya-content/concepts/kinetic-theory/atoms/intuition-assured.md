$\frac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle=\frac{3}{2}kT$ is the mean *translational* kinetic energy of one molecule — always true, for any ideal gas, monatomic or not. Finding a gas's total internal energy needs one more fact this formula alone does not supply.

A monatomic gas (helium, argon) truly has only three degrees of freedom — motion along $x$, $y$, $z$ — so its molar internal energy is exactly $U=\frac{3}{2}nRT$. A diatomic gas ($N_2$, $O_2$) has that same translational energy *plus* two rotational degrees of freedom (spinning about either axis perpendicular to the bond; spin about the bond itself carries negligible energy and does not count), each contributing another $\frac{1}{2}kT$ per molecule by the equipartition theorem. Its molar internal energy is $U=\frac{5}{2}nRT$ — a genuinely different number, not a rounding choice.

Writing $U=\frac{3}{2}nRT$ for nitrogen or oxygen is a specific, recurring error: it silently drops both rotational terms, undercounting the true internal energy by a full $nRT$ every mole.
