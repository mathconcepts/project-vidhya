Take a $0.16\text{ kg}$ ball, at rest, sped up to $30\text{ m/s}$.

Final kinetic energy: $\tfrac{1}{2}(0.16)(30)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.16)(900) = 72\text{ J}$.

It started with zero kinetic energy, so the change in kinetic energy is $72 - 0 = 72\text{ J}$. By the **work-energy theorem**, net work done on the ball equals that change: $72\text{ J}$ of work, done over an arm-swing of $1.5\text{ m}$.

Average force: $72 \div 1.5 = 48\text{ N}$.

That is what "work" buys you here — a real force number, computed from energy, without ever needing to track the arm's exact push at every instant. **Power** is how fast that $72\text{ J}$ was delivered — a separate number, not read off the work at all.
