- **Calling the same-named electrode positive or negative without checking the cell type**: in a galvanic cell, the anode (oxidation) is negative and the cathode (reduction) is positive, because the reaction runs on its own and pushes electrons OUT through the anode. In an electrolytic cell, an external battery forces electrons in the opposite direction, so the anode is now positive and the cathode is negative — oxidation and reduction still happen at the same two places, only the sign of each terminal flips.

- **Using the wrong $n$ in the Nernst equation or Faraday's law**: $n$ must be the number of electrons transferred per formula unit of the balanced reaction — not per atom of metal, and not assumed to be $1$ out of habit. Depositing $\text{Cu}$ from $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ needs $n=2$; using $n=1$ silently halves (or doubles) every answer that depends on it.

- **Assuming a positive $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ means the reaction is fast**: it only means the reaction is thermodynamically favourable. Whether it actually proceeds at a noticeable rate is a separate question about activation energy, not about $E^\circ_{\text{cell}}$ at all.

- **Forgetting that solids and pure liquids drop out of $Q$**: only dissolved ions (and gases, by their pressure) belong in the reaction quotient used in the Nernst equation. Including a solid electrode's "concentration" in $Q$ is meaningless — a pure solid's activity is defined as $1$.

- **Balancing a redox equation by atoms alone, ignoring charge**: matching every atom on both sides is necessary but not sufficient — the total charge must also balance, since electrons lost by one species must exactly equal electrons gained by the other. A charge-balance check catches errors an atom-count check alone misses.
