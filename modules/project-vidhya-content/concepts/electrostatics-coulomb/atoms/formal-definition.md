**Coulomb's law**: the force between two point charges $q_1, q_2$ a distance $r$ apart is $F = \dfrac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2}$, where $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 9\times10^9\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{C}^2$ ($\varepsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space, a fixed constant). Like charges repel, unlike charges attract; the direction always lies along the line joining the two charges.

**Electric field**: $\vec E = \vec F/q_0$ — the force per unit positive test charge $q_0$ at a point. Field of a single point charge $q$: $E = kq/r^2$, directed away from a positive $q$ and towards a negative $q$.

**Superposition**: the net field (or force) from several charges is the vector sum of each charge's own individual field (or force) — never add magnitudes without direction.

**Gauss's law**: $\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A = \dfrac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\varepsilon_0}$, where $Q_{\text{enc}}$ is the total charge enclosed by the closed surface (a "Gaussian surface") — a symmetry argument is what makes this solvable in one line.

**Electric potential**: $V = kq/r$ for a point charge; work done bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to that point.

**Potential energy of two charges**: $U = kq_1q_2/r$.

**Conductor in electrostatic equilibrium**: the electric field inside the conductor's material is exactly zero, and just outside its surface $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (perpendicular to the surface), where $\sigma$ is the local surface charge density.
