A weak acid like $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ (acetic acid, the acid in vinegar) never fully splits into ions in water. Instead it settles at a balance point: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$, where the rate of splitting apart equals the rate of recombining. This balance is captured by the **acid dissociation constant** $K_a = \dfrac{[\text{H}^+][\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-]}{[\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}]}$ — a fixed number, at a given temperature, that says how far the balance sits towards the split-apart side. A large $K_a$ means a strong pull towards splitting; a tiny one, like acetic acid's $1.8\times10^{-5}$, means only a small fraction of molecules actually ionise.

The **common-ion effect** is what happens when more of one of the products is added on purpose — say, sodium acetate, which supplies extra $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ ion — to that same equilibrium. Le Chatelier's principle (a system at balance, pushed one way by an outside change, shifts back the other way to resist the push) says the extra $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ pushes the balance back towards un-ionised $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$, so less of the acid ionises than it would alone. This is exactly how a **buffer** gets built: mix a weak acid with a salt of its own conjugate base, deliberately, to hold $[\text{H}^+]$ — and so the pH — nearly fixed.
