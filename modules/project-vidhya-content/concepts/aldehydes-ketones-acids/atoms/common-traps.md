- **Forgetting to check for an alpha-hydrogen before naming the reaction**: jumping straight to "aldol" for any carbonyl compound treated with base, without first checking whether an alpha-hydrogen (a hydrogen on the carbon next to the carbonyl carbon) is even available. No alpha-hydrogen means no enolate, and the reaction is Cannizzaro instead — the check has to come first, every time.

- **Assuming ketones give a positive Tollens' test**: only aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent ($\text{Ag(NH}_3)_2^+$) to metallic silver — a ketone's carbonyl carbon, crowded by two alkyl groups instead of at most one, does not get oxidised under these mild conditions. A positive silver-mirror test tells you "aldehyde", never "aldehyde or ketone".

- **Treating "more alkyl groups" as always slower, with no exceptions**: this is true for simple steric/electronic crowding (aldehyde faster than ketone), but an aromatic ring attached to the carbonyl carbon (as in acetophenone) slows nucleophilic addition further still, through resonance donation into the carbonyl group — a separate electronic effect layered on top of, not replacing, the steric one.

- **Confusing acidity of carboxylic acids with acidity of phenols or alcohols**: all three lose a proton, but only the carboxylate ion spreads its negative charge equally over two oxygens by resonance. This is why carboxylic acids are noticeably more acidic than phenols, which are in turn more acidic than plain alcohols — mixing up which resonance structure belongs to which compound is a frequent source of wrong acidity-ranking answers.

- **Miscounting which group raises or lowers acidity of a carboxylic acid**: an electron-withdrawing group on the $\text{R}$ chain (like a chlorine) pulls electron density away from the carboxylate and INCREASES acidity by spreading its charge further; an electron-donating alkyl group does the opposite and DECREASES it. Getting this backwards flips every substituent-effect ranking question.
