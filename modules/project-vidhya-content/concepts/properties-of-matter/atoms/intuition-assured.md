$\sigma=Y\epsilon$ needs no reintroduction here. The one thing worth double-checking on exam day: $Y$ is a genuine material constant only **within the elastic limit** — beyond it, stress and strain stop being proportional at all, and using $Y$ there gives a number with no physical meaning.

Counterexample: two identical steel wires, same $Y=2\times10^{11}\text{ Pa}$, one stretched by a small load well inside the elastic limit and one stretched by a load large enough to permanently deform it. A student who computes "expected strain" for the second wire using $\epsilon=\sigma/Y$ gets a number smaller than the wire's *actual*, permanent strain — because past the elastic limit, real strain grows faster than $Y$ predicts, and some of it never reverses even when the load is removed. $Y$ answers "how stiff is this material in its elastic range", never "how much will this specific wire permanently stretch under a large enough load".
