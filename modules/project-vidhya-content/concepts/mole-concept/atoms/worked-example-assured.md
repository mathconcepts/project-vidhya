**Problem:** 4 g of $\text{H}_2$ gas is mixed with 16 g of $\text{O}_2$ gas and ignited: $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Which reactant is the limiting reagent, and what mass of water forms?

---

**Step 1 — Moles, not masses, decide the limiting reagent.** Moles $\text{H}_2 = 4/2 = 2$; moles $\text{O}_2 = 16/32 = 0.5$. Comparing 4 g to 16 g directly (or even 2 mol to 0.5 mol directly, ignoring the 2:1 ratio) is the trap — the reagent with the smaller mass, or even the smaller mole count, is not automatically limiting.

---

**Step 2 — Divide each mole count by its own coefficient, then compare.** $\text{H}_2$: $2/2 = 1$. $\text{O}_2$: $0.5/1 = 0.5$. The smaller result, $\text{O}_2$'s 0.5, identifies $\text{O}_2$ as limiting — this division-by-coefficient method scales to any two-reactant problem without re-deriving the ratio each time.

---

**Step 3 — Product from the limiting reagent's moles.** $0.5$ mol $\text{O}_2 \times 2 = 1$ mol $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18$ g.

$$\boxed{\text{O}_2 \text{ limiting; } 18\text{ g H}_2\text{O}}$$

A genuinely non-obvious counterexample: if instead 16 g $\text{H}_2$ (8 mol) met 16 g $\text{O}_2$ (0.5 mol), $\text{H}_2$'s divided value ($8/2=4$) still exceeds $\text{O}_2$'s ($0.5/1=0.5$) — $\text{O}_2$ stays limiting even though its mass equals $\text{H}_2$'s.
