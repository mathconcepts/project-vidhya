A rower pushes an oar backward against the water. The water, in turn, pushes the oar (and through it, the boat) forward — the exact same pair of forces Newton's third law describes, just acted out with a paddle instead of a string and pulley. Neither push exists without the other, and each one acts on a *different* object: the oar pushes the water, the water pushes the boat. Drawing a free-body diagram for the boat alone must include only the water's push *on the boat*; the boat's own push on the water belongs on a completely separate diagram for the water, never on the boat's.

The same rower, gliding forward with no more paddling, keeps moving at a steady speed until water resistance (a real, if small, opposing force) eventually slows the boat down — Newton's first law, showing up as "nothing keeps a moving object going forever" only because *some* net force, however small, is usually acting to stop it, not because motion needs a constant push to continue.
