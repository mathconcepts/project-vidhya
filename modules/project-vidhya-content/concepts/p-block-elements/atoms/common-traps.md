- **Assuming halogen bond strength falls smoothly down the group**: the memorised trend says bond strength should just decrease as atoms get bigger, but $F-F$ (159 kJ/mol) is weaker than $Cl-Cl$ (242 kJ/mol) — right where a question about "which halogen has the strongest bond" is likely to test the exception, not the trend. Small size cuts both ways: it should help orbital overlap, but it also crams three lone pairs on each atom close enough to repel across the bond.

- **Confusing a weak bond with low reactivity**: $F_2$ has the weakest bond of Cl, Br, F (excluding I), yet fluorine is the *most* reactive halogen of all. A weak bond breaks easily, which speeds a reaction up rather than slowing it down — bond strength and reactivity are not the same axis.

- **Picking the wrong central atom in an interhalogen**: the central atom is always the larger, less electronegative halogen, never the smaller one. In $ClF_3$, chlorine is central even though fluorine is more electronegative — electronegativity decides bond polarity, not who sits in the middle.

- **Treating every noble gas as equally capable of forming compounds**: only xenon (and, in far rarer cases, krypton) forms real, isolable compounds with fluorine or oxygen. Helium, neon and argon do not — their ionisation enthalpies are too high and their outer shells too tightly held for any known partner to pull electron density away.

- **Assuming the inert pair effect belongs only to Group 13**: it shows up in every one of groups 13, 14 and 15 wherever the heaviest member is reached — thallium ($Tl^+$ over $Tl^{3+}$), lead ($Pb^{2+}$ over $Pb^{4+}$) and bismuth (its $+5$ compounds are strong oxidisers, wanting to fall back to $+3$).
