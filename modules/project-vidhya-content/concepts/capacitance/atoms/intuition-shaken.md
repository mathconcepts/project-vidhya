Take $C_1=6\ \mu\text{F}$ and $C_2=3\ \mu\text{F}$, connected in series (one after another, same charge $Q$ flows onto both).

Series formula: $\dfrac{1}{C_s}=\dfrac{1}{C_1}+\dfrac{1}{C_2}=\dfrac{1}{6}+\dfrac{1}{3}=\dfrac{1}{2}$, so $C_s=2\ \mu\text{F}$.

Notice: $2\ \mu\text{F}$ is *smaller* than either $C_1$ or $C_2$ alone — the opposite of what happens with resistors, where series values always add up to something bigger.

Now connect the same two side by side instead (parallel, same voltage across both): $C_p=C_1+C_2=6+3=9\ \mu\text{F}$ — bigger than either one, because both plates' worth of charge-holding room is now available at the same time.

Series shrinks capacitance below the smallest value. Parallel grows it beyond the largest value. That is the whole rule, checked with real numbers.
