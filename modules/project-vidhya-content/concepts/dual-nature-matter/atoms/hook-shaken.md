Shine light of wavelength $400$ nanometres (a violet-blue colour) onto a sodium plate. Sodium's **work function** — the least energy needed to pull one electron free of the metal — is $2.3$ eV, where eV (electron volt) is a small, convenient unit of energy. Using $E=1240/\lambda(\text{nm})$ eV, this light delivers $E=1240/400=3.1$ eV to each electron it hits.

Leftover kinetic energy for the electron: $3.1-2.3=0.8$ eV. Now make the same light ten times brighter — ten times as many light packets, called **photons**, hit the plate each second, so ten times as many electrons fly out. Each one still leaves with exactly $0.8$ eV, because each photon still delivers exactly $3.1$ eV; brightness never entered that subtraction at all.

This is the **photoelectric effect**: light knocks electrons out of a metal (the ejected electrons are called photoelectrons), and only the light's colour — its **frequency**, how many light-wave cycles pass a point each second — decides their top energy, never its brightness.
