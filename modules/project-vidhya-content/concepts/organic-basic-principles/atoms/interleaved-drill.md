**Cross-concept check: organic-basic-principles → hydrocarbons.**

**Question 1 (which carbocation forms, and why):** Propene, $\text{CH}_3\text{-CH=CH}_2$, reacts with $\text{HBr}$ with no peroxide present. Addition puts $\text{H}^+$ on one end of the double bond first, forming a carbocation before bromide attacks. Which carbon gets the positive charge — the middle one or the end one — and why?

*Answer:* The middle carbon. Adding $\text{H}^+$ to the end carbon (carbon 1) leaves the positive charge on the middle carbon (carbon 2), which is joined to two other carbons — secondary. Adding $\text{H}^+$ to the middle carbon instead would leave the charge on carbon 1, joined to only one other carbon — primary. The secondary carbocation is more stable (more neighbouring $\text{C-H}$ bonds to hyperconjugate), so the reaction goes that way, and bromide then attacks the middle carbon.

**Question 2 (name the rule this reaction result is an example of):** What is this outcome called, and what does it predict for the product?

*Answer:* **Markovnikov's rule** — the halogen ends up on the more substituted carbon, because that is exactly where the more stable, more heavily hyperconjugated carbocation forms first. The product is 2-bromopropane, not 1-bromopropane.

**Why this drill exists:** the carbocation-stability reasoning from this concept is not a one-off puzzle about a rearranging bromide — it is the exact same reasoning that predicts which product an alkene addition reaction gives, which is the next concept's central rule.
