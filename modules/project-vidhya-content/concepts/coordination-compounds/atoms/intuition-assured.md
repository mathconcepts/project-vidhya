Assuming VBT and CFT are interchangeable once you know the hybridisation and the spin state is the mark-costing trap — CFT computes something VBT was never built to touch: a genuine number, crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE), and that number predicts behaviour VBT cannot.

Counterexample: low-spin $d^6$ complexes like $[Co(NH_3)_6]^{3+}$ are famous for being kinetically inert — they resist ligand-exchange reactions for hours or longer, unlike most other complexes, which exchange ligands in fractions of a second. VBT's static hybridisation label, $d^2sp^3$, gives no reason to expect this at all; it only describes a shape and a spin state. CFT explains it: a fully filled lower $d$ level in a $d^6$ low-spin complex has an unusually large CFSE, meaning any intermediate state formed while a ligand is leaving or arriving is energetically expensive to reach, so the reaction is slow. "Which theory to use" is not a matter of preference; VBT and CFT are simply asked different questions, and only one of them was ever built to answer this one.
