A **nucleon** (a proton or a neutron) is strongly held only by its closest neighbours — the **nuclear force** that binds a nucleus together reaches just a very short distance, not the whole nucleus at once.

In a very small nucleus (like helium, $4$ nucleons), a large share of the nucleons sit on the "surface", with fewer neighbours holding onto them — average binding per nucleon is modest. In a medium-sized nucleus (like iron, $56$ nucleons), almost every nucleon is surrounded by neighbours on every side — average binding per nucleon peaks here. In a very large nucleus (like uranium, $235$ nucleons), the growing pile of protons all repel each other electrically across the *whole* nucleus (unlike the nuclear force, this electric repulsion does reach that far) — the extra push-back drags the average binding back down a little.

That is the whole binding-energy-per-nucleon curve, in one line: it rises while adding neighbours helps more than adding protons hurts, peaks around iron, and falls slowly afterward as proton-proton repulsion takes over.
