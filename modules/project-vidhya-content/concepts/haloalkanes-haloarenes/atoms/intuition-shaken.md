Follow one primary molecule, step by step. 1-bromobutane, bromine on a carbon touching only one other carbon.

Step 1: a nucleophile's lone pair approaches from the side directly opposite the bromine — the only open side, since nothing else is crowding that carbon.

Step 2: the nucleophile-carbon bond forms and the bond to bromine snaps at the same instant, in one single step. This is $S_N2$.

Step 3: because the attack came from the back, the carbon's other three groups flip to the opposite side — like an umbrella turning inside out. The product's spatial arrangement is now the mirror image of the starting one.

Now switch to a tertiary molecule instead: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, bromine on a carbon touching three other carbons. Backside attack has nowhere to go — three groups block it. So the bromine leaves *first*, alone, forming a flat carbocation. The nucleophile only then attacks, from either face equally. This is $S_N1$, and the product is a roughly equal mix of both spatial arrangements, not a single flipped one.
