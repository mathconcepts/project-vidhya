**Problem:** tert-butyl methyl ether, $\text{CH}_3\text{-O-C(CH}_3)_3$, is heated with excess $\text{HI}$. Two carbons are attached to the ether oxygen: a methyl carbon and a tertiary carbon. Which carbon-oxygen bond breaks, and by which mechanism?

---

**Step 1 — Name the two candidate pathways.** Either iodide attacks the unhindered methyl carbon directly, by backside attack ($S_N2$), or the tertiary carbon ionises on its own first, forming a carbocation that iodide then attacks ($S_N1$). A quick guess — "attack the smaller, less crowded carbon" — points at the methyl carbon and $S_N2$.

---

**Step 2 — Check whether that guess actually wins.** The tertiary carbon is attached to three methyl groups. If it lost the oxygen first, the resulting carbocation would be a tertiary one — stabilised by hyperconjugation from nine surrounding $\text{C-H}$ bonds and by the inductive effect from three neighbouring carbons. That carbocation is stable enough to form readily on its own, without needing iodide to push it out.

---

**Step 3 — Compare the two carbons directly.** The methyl carbon, if it lost the oxygen instead, would form a methyl cation — essentially the least stable carbocation possible, with no neighbouring carbons to help at all. Forming *that* carbocation is far too costly; ionising the tertiary carbon is much easier.

---

**Step 4 — Decide the mechanism.** Because a comfortable tertiary carbocation is available, the reaction takes the $S_N1$ route at the tertiary carbon instead of the naive $S_N2$ guess at the methyl carbon.

---

**Step 5 — State the outcome.** $\boxed{\text{the tertiary carbon-oxygen bond breaks by } S_N1\text{, giving tert-butyl iodide and methanol}}$ — not methyl iodide and tert-butanol, which is what the "attack the smaller carbon" guess would have predicted.
