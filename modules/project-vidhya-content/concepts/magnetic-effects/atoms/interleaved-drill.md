**Cross-concept check: magnetic-effects → electromagnetic-induction.**

**Question 1 (a field, sitting still):** A uniform magnetic field $B=0.2\ \text{T}$ passes straight through a flat loop of area $A=0.05\ \text{m}^2$, perpendicular to the loop's plane. Find the magnetic flux through the loop.

*Answer:* $\Phi=BA=0.2\times0.05=0.01\ \text{Wb}=10\ \text{mWb}$ ($\text{Wb}$, weber, is the unit of magnetic flux). Nothing here is moving — this is just a static field passing through a fixed area.

**Question 2 (now move the loop):** If this same loop is suddenly pulled out of the field region, does anything electrical happen, even though no battery or cell is anywhere in sight?

*Answer:* Yes — an emf appears in the loop, because the flux $\Phi=BA$ through it is now *changing* (dropping from $10\ \text{mWb}$ towards $0$ as the loop leaves the field). This changing flux is exactly what induces a current, with no cell needed anywhere in the circuit.

**Why this drill exists:** magnetic-effects treats the field as something a moving charge or current reacts to. The instant the loop itself starts moving relative to the field, the flux through it changes, and a completely new effect appears — one that magnetic-effects' own formulas say nothing about, but which follows directly from the same field this concept describes.
