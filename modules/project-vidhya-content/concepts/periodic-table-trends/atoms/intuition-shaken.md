Take three real numbers from Period 2, in kilojoules per mole: lithium 520, beryllium 899, boron 801. From lithium to beryllium, enthalpy rises: 899 is bigger than 520. From beryllium to boron, it falls: 801 is smaller than 899. That fall is real and it has a structural cause.

Beryllium's electron configuration is $1s^22s^2$ — its outer subshell, $2s^2$, is completely filled. A filled subshell is extra stable, so pulling an electron out of it costs extra energy. Boron's configuration is $1s^22s^22p^1$ — its outermost electron sits alone in a $2p$ orbital, which lies at slightly higher energy than $2s$ and is shielded a little by the $2s^2$ pair underneath it. So boron's newest electron is easier to remove than beryllium's, even though boron has one more proton. That is why 801 is less than 899.

The same pattern repeats between nitrogen (1402) and oxygen (1314). Nitrogen's configuration ends in $2p^3$ — three electrons, one in each of the three $2p$ orbitals, no pairing at all (extra stable, called half-filled stability). Oxygen's configuration ends in $2p^4$ — the fourth electron must pair up with one already in an orbital, and two electrons crammed into one orbital repel each other. That extra repulsion makes oxygen's paired electron easier to pull out than any of nitrogen's three lone ones, so 1314 is less than 1402.

Outside these two specific gaps — $2s$ to $2p$, and half-filled $2p^3$ to paired $2p^4$ — the rise across a period is unbroken. Down a group, check sodium (496) against lithium (520): sodium's value is lower, because its outer electron sits in a whole new, farther-out shell, and that one change outweighs sodium's extra protons every time.
