- **Treating every $-\text{OH}$ group as equally acidic**: an ordinary alcohol's alkoxide has nowhere to spread its negative charge; a phenoxide ion spreads it by resonance onto three ring carbons. That single structural difference, not anything about oxygen itself, is what makes phenol roughly a million times more acidic than an alcohol.

- **Picking the smaller, less hindered carbon for ether cleavage without checking for a tertiary carbon first**: with a tertiary alkyl group present, cleavage switches to $S_N1$ at that carbon, giving the *opposite* product pairing to what "attack the less crowded carbon" would predict.

- **Mixing up Reimer-Tiemann and Kolbe's reaction**: both start from phenol (or sodium phenoxide) and both put a new group ortho to the $-\text{OH}$. Reimer-Tiemann (chloroform $+$ $\text{NaOH}$) installs $-\text{CHO}$; Kolbe's reaction (sodium phenoxide $+$ $\text{CO}_2$) installs $-\text{COOH}$. Confusing the reagent with the product is an easy slip under time pressure.

- **Forgetting that a bulky $\text{R}'\text{X}$ ruins Williamson ether synthesis**: the alkoxide $\text{RO}^-$ is a strong base as well as a nucleophile. With a secondary or, worse, tertiary $\text{R}'\text{X}$, it pulls off a hydrogen and causes elimination far more readily than it substitutes.

- **Assuming any electron-donating group anywhere on the ring lowers phenol's acidity by the same amount**: the effect is far weaker at the meta position than at ortho or para, because only ortho and para carbons carry the phenoxide ion's delocalised negative charge.
