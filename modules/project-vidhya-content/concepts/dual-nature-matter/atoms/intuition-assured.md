The formula $\lambda=h/p=h/\sqrt{2meV}$, often shortened to $\lambda\approx12.27/\sqrt{V}$ angstrom, bakes in one silent assumption: that $m$ is an **electron's** mass. Swap in a different accelerated particle and that shortcut number stops being true.

Counterexample: accelerate a proton (mass about $1836$ times an electron's) through the same $100$ volts used for an electron. The electron's **de Broglie wavelength** (the wavelength associated with a moving object) comes out $\approx1.227$ angstrom; the proton's works out to $\approx0.0286$ angstrom — about $43$ times smaller, exactly $\sqrt{1836}$, not the same $12.27/\sqrt{V}$ number at all. The general relation $\lambda=h/p$ is universal; the convenient $12.27/\sqrt{V}$ shortcut is not — it only ever holds for an electron.

One more distinction worth holding onto: this formula needs **momentum**, not raw kinetic energy. For any charged particle sped up through potential $V$, momentum comes from $p=\sqrt{2mqV}$, using that particle's own mass $m$ and charge $q$ — never the electron's numbers, borrowed out of habit.
