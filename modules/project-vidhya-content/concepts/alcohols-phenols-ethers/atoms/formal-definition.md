**Acidity order among alcohols**: tertiary $<$ secondary $<$ primary $<$ methanol. More alkyl carbons around the $-\text{OH}$ push more electron density onto the oxygen (through the inductive effect), destabilising the negative alkoxide ion that forms when the proton leaves — so more branching means less acidic.

**Phenol versus alcohols**: phenol ($\text{p}K_a \approx 10$) is far more acidic than an ordinary alcohol ($\text{p}K_a \approx 16$-$18$), because the phenoxide ion's negative charge delocalises by resonance onto the ring's ortho and para carbons, spreading it out and lowering the ion's energy. An alkoxide ion has no such ring to delocalise into.

**Substituent effects on phenol's acidity**: an electron-withdrawing group (e.g. $-\text{NO}_2$) at the *ortho* or *para* position increases acidity further, since it sits on one of the phenoxide ion's own charge-bearing resonance carbons and pulls on that charge directly. The same group at *meta* helps only through the weaker inductive effect, since meta carbons never carry the resonance charge. An electron-donating group (e.g. $-\text{OCH}_3$, an alkyl group) at ortho or para *decreases* acidity, by pushing additional electron density onto an already-negative ion.

**Reimer-Tiemann reaction**: phenol, treated with chloroform and $\text{NaOH}$, forms a $-\text{CHO}$ group at the position ortho to the $-\text{OH}$ (giving salicylaldehyde), via an electrophilic carbon species called dichlorocarbene attacking the electron-rich ring.

**Kolbe's reaction**: sodium phenoxide, treated with carbon dioxide under pressure, forms a $-\text{COOH}$ group ortho to the $-\text{OH}$ (giving salicylic acid), because the ring position ortho to the electron-donating oxide is especially electron-rich and attacks the carbon of $\text{CO}_2$ directly.

**Williamson ether synthesis**: an alkoxide ion, $\text{RO}^-$, displaces a leaving group from a second alkyl halide, $\text{R}'\text{X}$, forming an ether $\text{R-O-R}'$ by backside attack ($S_N2$). Works best when $\text{R}'\text{X}$ is primary; with a secondary or tertiary $\text{R}'\text{X}$, the strongly basic alkoxide tends to cause elimination instead of substitution.

**Ether cleavage by excess $\text{HX}$**: if both alkyl groups on the ether are primary or secondary, the halide ion attacks the less hindered carbon by $S_N2$. If one alkyl group is tertiary, the mechanism switches entirely to $S_N1$: that carbon ionises to a stable tertiary carbocation first, and the halide attacks it there instead — even though a naive "attack the smaller group" guess would point to the other carbon.
