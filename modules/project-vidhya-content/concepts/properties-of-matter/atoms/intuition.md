Stretch a wire and it resists — that resistance is captured by **stress** (force per unit area, $\sigma=F/A$) and **strain** (fractional change in length, $\epsilon=\Delta L/L_0$, a pure number with no unit). **Hooke's law** says stress is proportional to strain, $\sigma = Y\epsilon$, where **Young's modulus** $Y$ is a material property — steel has a high $Y$ (stiff, resists stretching), rubber has a low one (stretches easily for the same stress).

This proportionality is **not universal** — it holds only within the **elastic limit**, the point up to which a stretched material springs back to its original length once the force is removed. Push a wire past that limit and $\sigma=Y\epsilon$ stops applying at all; the material deforms permanently, and eventually breaks.

A steel wire ($1\text{ mm}^2$ cross-section, $1\text{ m}$ long) stretched by $1\text{ mm}$ under a $200\text{ N}$ load: stress $=200/10^{-6}=2\times10^8\text{ Pa}$, strain $=10^{-3}/1=10^{-3}$, so $Y = \sigma/\epsilon = 2\times10^{11}\text{ Pa}$ — the real, textbook value for steel, confirming this small stretch is well inside the elastic limit.
