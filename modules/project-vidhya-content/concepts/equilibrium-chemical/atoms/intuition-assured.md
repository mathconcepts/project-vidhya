Le Chatelier's Principle is a **prediction rule**, not a mechanism — it correctly says *which way* an equilibrium shifts under a stress, but says nothing about *why*, at the level of actual reaction rates.

The real mechanism (why the shift happens at all): a stress changes the forward and reverse rates by *different* amounts, temporarily making $Q \ne K$ again, and the reaction runs in whichever direction restores $Q=K$. A concentration change is the clean case — Le Chatelier's rule and this rate-based mechanism always agree there.

The case where relying on Le Chatelier alone gets genuinely risky: temperature changes for an *exothermic* reaction, since raising $T$ shifts the equilibrium position (Le Chatelier: away from the heat-releasing direction) **and** changes the value of $K$ itself — the only kind of stress that alters $K$, rather than merely displacing $Q$ away from an unchanged $K$. Concentration, pressure, and volume changes shift the position without ever touching $K$'s actual value; temperature is the one exception.
