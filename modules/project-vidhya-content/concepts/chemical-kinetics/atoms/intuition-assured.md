Reading a reaction's order straight off its balanced equation's coefficients is the trap; the true source of order is whichever step is slowest in its actual mechanism.

$2\text{N}_2\text{O}_5 \rightarrow 4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2$ has a "$2$" in front of $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ — a value that might suggest second-order behaviour, or a molecularity of $2$. Experimentally, though, this reaction is FIRST order: $\text{rate}=k[\text{N}_2\text{O}_5]$. The reason is mechanistic: the reaction proceeds through several elementary steps, and the slowest one (the "rate-determining step") is the unimolecular decomposition of a single $\text{N}_2\text{O}_5$ molecule — molecularity $1$ — which is exactly what the measured order of $1$ reflects.

**The overall balanced equation's coefficients are neither the order nor the molecularity of anything** — they only balance atoms. Order comes from the slowest step's own rate law; molecularity is a property of one single elementary step, never of the equation summarising several steps combined.
