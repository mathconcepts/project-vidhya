Take 0.1 mol/L acetic acid on its own. Its ionisation is small, so approximately $[\text{H}^+] \approx \sqrt{K_a \cdot C} = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-5} \times 0.1} = 1.34\times10^{-3}$ mol/L. That gives $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(1.34\times10^{-3}) = 2.87$.

Now add enough sodium acetate to also make $[\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-] = 0.1$ mol/L, without adding any more acid. This extra $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ is the **common ion** — it is already one of the acid's own products.

Rearrange $K_a = [\text{H}^+][\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-]/[\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}]$: $[\text{H}^+] = K_a \times [\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}]/[\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-] = 1.8\times10^{-5} \times (0.1/0.1) = 1.8\times10^{-5}$ mol/L.

Check: $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}(1.8\times10^{-5}) = 4.74$ — up from $2.87$. The common ion pushed the equilibrium back towards the un-ionised acid, exactly as Le Chatelier's principle predicts, and cut $[\text{H}^+]$ to roughly $1/75$ of its earlier value.
