- **Getting the contact-angle sign backwards for capillary rise**: water in a clean glass tube has a contact angle $\theta<90°$ (wets the glass), so $\cos\theta>0$ and the liquid **rises**. Mercury has $\theta>90°$ (does not wet glass), so $\cos\theta<0$ and the liquid is **depressed** instead. Using the rise formula $h=2T\cos\theta/(\rho gr)$ without checking whether $\cos\theta$ is actually positive or negative for the given liquid produces the wrong direction, not just the wrong number.

- **Applying Bernoulli's equation when its assumptions are silently broken**: the equation $P+\tfrac12\rho v^2+\rho gh=\text{constant}$ needs steady, incompressible, non-viscous flow **along one streamline**. A genuinely viscous fluid (thick oil, for instance) loses energy to friction along the pipe, so Bernoulli's equation alone will overestimate the actual downstream pressure or speed — the "constant" quietly stops being constant.

- **Confusing stress (a material property calculation) with pressure (a fluid quantity)**: both are force per unit area and share the pascal as a unit, but stress in a stretched solid and pressure in a fluid are governed by entirely different rules — Hooke's law has no buoyancy term, and Pascal's law has no Young's modulus. Sharing a unit does not mean sharing a formula.

- **Forgetting that $\Delta P=4T/r$ (not $2T/r$) applies to a soap bubble**: a bubble has *two* liquid surfaces (inner and outer), each contributing $2T/r$ of excess pressure, doubling the single-surface result used for a plain liquid drop.

- **Assuming Young's modulus keeps working past the elastic limit**: $\sigma=Y\epsilon$ is a straight-line relationship valid only up to the elastic limit. Using it to predict strain for a heavily overloaded wire gives a smaller number than the wire's real, permanently-deformed strain.
