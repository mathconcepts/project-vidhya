$K_a$ stays fixed at a given temperature; the **degree of ionisation** $\alpha$ (the fraction of acid molecules that actually split apart) does not, and treating the two as interchangeable is where marks are lost.

For a weak acid, $\alpha \approx \sqrt{K_a/C}$ (from $K_a \approx C\alpha^2$ at small $\alpha$). Dilute the same $0.1$ M acetic acid ten-fold, to $0.01$ M: $K_a$ stays $1.8\times10^{-5}$, unchanged, but $\alpha$ rises from about $0.0134$ to about $0.0424$ — more than three times higher.

So "a fixed $K_a$ means a fixed $[\text{H}^+]$" is false: $[\text{H}^+]=\sqrt{K_aC}$ still falls with dilution, from $1.34\times10^{-3}$ to $4.24\times10^{-4}$ mol/L, even though the ionised *fraction* rose. A constant $K_a$ fixes the relationship between concentration and ionisation — never the ionisation itself. This is Ostwald's dilution law, and it is exactly the "constant vs. variable" swap JEE distractors are built on.
