When phenol loses its $-\text{OH}$ hydrogen as $\text{H}^+$, the leftover negative charge does not stay parked on the oxygen. That oxygen's extra lone pair joins the ring's own connected pi system by resonance, spreading the negative charge out onto three ring carbons (the ones ortho and para to the oxygen) as well as staying on the oxygen itself. Spreading one charge across four positions instead of squeezing it onto one lowers the ion's energy substantially — a spread-out charge is a calmer, lower-energy charge. That calmer phenoxide ion is easier to form, which is exactly what "more acidic" means: readier to give up the proton that produces it.

An ordinary alcohol's alkoxide ion, $\text{RO}^-$, has no ring to spread into — the negative charge sits entirely on one oxygen, high in energy, reluctant to form. Worse, the alkyl group $\text{R}$ pushes *extra* electron density onto that already-negative oxygen through the inductive effect, piling more charge onto the one spot instead of relieving it — which is why a bulkier alkyl group (more carbons pushing harder) makes an alcohol even less acidic, not more.
