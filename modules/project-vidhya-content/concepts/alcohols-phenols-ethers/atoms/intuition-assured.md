Assuming an electron-withdrawing substituent boosts a phenol's acidity by roughly the same amount from every ring position badly misjudges where its help actually lands.

Counterexample: p-nitrophenol (nitro directly opposite the $-\text{OH}$) has a $\text{p}K_a$ around $7.2$ — far more acidic than plain phenol. m-nitrophenol (nitro one position off) has a $\text{p}K_a$ around $8.3$ — still more acidic than phenol, but noticeably less than its para isomer, despite carrying the identical nitro group.

The reason is structural, not a matter of "more electronegative equals more effect": the phenoxide ion's resonance structures put negative charge specifically on the oxygen and the ortho and para ring carbons. A nitro group sitting at *para* sits right where that charge already concentrates, and pulls on it directly by resonance. A nitro group at *meta* never sits on any of those charge-bearing carbons — it can only help through the weaker inductive effect, reaching through the sigma-bond framework instead.

Position relative to those specific resonance carbons decides the size of the effect, not merely which group is attached.
