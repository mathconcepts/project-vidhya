A water strider walks on a pond without breaking through, because water's surface behaves like a thin, taut skin — **surface tension** ($T$, force per unit length) is that skin pulling itself as tight as possible, so a light-enough weight simply dents it instead of sinking through. Pull a small loop of thread out of soapy water and it snaps into a perfect circle for the same reason: the surface pulls inward on the thread from every direction it can, and a circle is the shape enclosing the most area for the least edge to pull along.

That same inward pull is why a thin glass tube dipped in water shows the water climbing up its own walls — **capillary rise**. The narrower the tube, the higher the climb, since the same pulling force acts on a proportionally smaller ring of contact at the tube's rim. Dip the same tube in mercury instead, and it pushes the liquid level *down* rather than up — mercury simply does not "wet" glass the way water does, so the pull acts in the opposite direction.
