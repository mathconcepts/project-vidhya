**Newton's first law**: an object keeps its current velocity (speed and direction) unless a net force acts on it. "At rest" and "moving at constant velocity" are treated identically by this law — there is no special preference for standing still.

**Newton's second law**: a net force $\vec{F}_{\text{net}}$ produces an acceleration $\vec{a}=\vec{F}_{\text{net}}/m$, in the *same* direction as the net force. This is the equation that actually solves problems: draw every force acting on one object, add them as vectors (respecting a chosen sign convention), and set the result equal to $ma$.

**Newton's third law**: forces come in pairs — if object $A$ pushes on object $B$, then $B$ pushes back on $A$ with equal magnitude and opposite direction. These two forces act on *different* objects, so they never cancel each other out in any single free-body diagram.

**A free-body diagram** isolates one object and draws only the forces acting *on* it (never the forces it exerts on other things), each with its own direction fixed by the chosen sign convention. This is the mandatory first picture before any equation for a connected-bodies or friction problem — skipping it is how forces get missed or double-counted.

**Friction**: a contact force opposing relative sliding (or the tendency to slide), with maximum static value $f_{s,\max}=\mu_s N$ and kinetic value $f_k=\mu_k N$, where $N$ is the normal (perpendicular contact) force and $\mu$ is the coefficient of friction for that pair of surfaces.
