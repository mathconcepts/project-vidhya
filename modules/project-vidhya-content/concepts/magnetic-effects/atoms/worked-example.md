**Problem:** A proton moves with speed $v=2\times10^6\ \text{m/s}$ along the $+x$ direction. A uniform magnetic field $B=0.5\ \text{T}$ points along the $+y$ direction, perpendicular to the proton's velocity. Find the magnitude and direction of the magnetic force on the proton, and state what the force would be instead if $\vec B$ pointed along $+x$.

---

**Step 1 — Describe the geometry.** Velocity is along $+x$. Field is along $+y$, at $90°$ to the velocity. This is the geometry that gives the largest possible force for this speed and field strength, since $\sin\theta$ is maximised at $\theta=90°$.

---

**Step 2 — Find the magnitude.** $F=qvB\sin\theta=(1.6\times10^{-19})(2\times10^6)(0.5)\sin90°$.

$$\boxed{F = 1.6\times10^{-13}\ \text{N}}$$

---

**Step 3 — Find the direction using the right-hand rule.** Curl the fingers of your right hand from $\vec v$ ($+x$) towards $\vec B$ ($+y$), through the smaller angle — the thumb points along $+z$ (out of the page, in the standard $x$-$y$-$z$ right-handed setup). Since the proton is positive, the force is along $+z$.

$$\boxed{\vec F \text{ points along } +z}$$

---

**Step 4 — Now change the geometry: $\vec B$ along $+x$ instead.** Velocity ($+x$) and field ($+x$) are now the **same** direction, so $\theta=0°$.

$$\boxed{F = qvB\sin0° = 0}$$

---

**Why the geometry, described first, decided everything.** Nothing about the charge, speed, or field strength changed between Step 2 and Step 4 — only the *angle* between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$ did, from $90°$ to $0°$. A moving charge feels the full force only when its velocity has a component perpendicular to the field; a velocity purely along the field contributes nothing to the force at all, however large $q$, $v$, or $B$ happen to be.
