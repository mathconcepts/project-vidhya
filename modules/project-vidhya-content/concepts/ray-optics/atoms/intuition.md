Keep the same convex lens, $f=20\text{ cm}$, but now move the object closer — to $10\text{ cm}$ in front of the lens, *inside* the focal length. Draw the two rays again: parallel-then-through-focus, and straight-through-centre. This time they never actually cross on the far side at all — they spread apart, moving away from each other. Only by extending them *backwards*, behind the object's own side of the lens, do they appear to meet.

The formula tracks this automatically through its sign. With $u=-10\text{ cm}$: $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{20}+\dfrac{1}{-10} = -\dfrac{1}{20}$, so $v=-20\text{ cm}$ — **negative** this time, meaning the "image" sits on the *same* side as the object, not the far side. That is exactly what a **virtual** image is: not a point where light rays actually cross, but a point where they only *appear* to have come from.

Compare this to the earlier case, object at $30\text{ cm}$: there, $v$ came out positive ($60\text{ cm}$, real, rays genuinely cross) and $m$ was negative ($-2$, inverted). Here, $v$ is negative (virtual) and $m=v/u=-20/-10=+2$ is positive (upright). The sign of $v$ tells you real-or-virtual; the sign of $m$ tells you upright-or-inverted — two separate pieces of information, from two separate signs, in the same one calculation.
