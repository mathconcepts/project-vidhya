Picture a stadium full of people leaving after a match, all funnelled onto the same road. A few stragglers walk out slowly, most people walk out at a comfortable, similar pace, and a handful jog to beat the traffic. Plot "number of people" against "walking speed" and the shape is not symmetric — it rises quickly from zero, peaks somewhere in the middle, then trails off slowly to the right, because there is no upper limit on how fast a person *could* jog, but everyone has to start from zero.

Gas molecules behave exactly like that crowd. The bar chart on this card shows, schematically, how many molecules (vertical bar height) move at each range of speeds (horizontal axis, in m/s) in a typical gas sample. Most molecules cluster around a middling speed; very few move extremely slowly, and a smaller-but-real fraction move much faster than the rest — the same right-skewed, single-peaked shape as the stadium crowd, for the same reason: nothing stops a molecule from being unusually fast, but every molecule's speed is bounded below by zero.
