Take nitrogen gas ($N_2$, molar mass $M=0.028$ kg/mol — the main gas in the air you breathe) at room temperature, $T=300$ K.

**Step 1 — the energy of one molecule.** $\frac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle=\frac{3}{2}kT=\frac{3}{2}(1.38\times10^{-23})(300)=6.21\times10^{-21}$ J. Tiny on its own — but a mole holds about $6\times10^{23}$ molecules, and they add up fast.

**Step 2 — how fast that energy makes a molecule move.** $v_{rms}=\sqrt{3RT/M}=\sqrt{3(8.314)(300)/0.028}\approx517$ m/s — faster than sound travels through air.

**Step 3 — swap in oxygen ($O_2$, $M=0.032$ kg/mol) at the same 300 K.** The energy per molecule, $\frac{3}{2}kT$, does not change at all — it never depended on mass. But $v_{rms}$ *does* depend on mass, and a heavier molecule needs less speed to carry the same energy: oxygen's rms speed comes out to about 484 m/s, lower than nitrogen's 517 m/s, even though both gases sit at the same temperature.

**Step 4 — where pressure actually comes from.** A wall does not "feel" kinetic energy directly. It feels billions of individual collisions every second, each one a tiny push. Add every push up, over one second, over one square metre of wall, and that total is exactly what a pressure gauge reads.

**Step 5 — the three typical speeds.** Most probable speed, average speed, and rms speed always sit in that order, smallest to largest, for every ideal gas at every temperature — never any other order.
