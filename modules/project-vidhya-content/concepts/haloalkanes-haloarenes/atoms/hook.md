Two bottles, both labelled "bromide, one carbon attached to bromine." One holds 1-bromobutane, $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Br}$, with the bromine on an end carbon touching only one other carbon. The other holds 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}$, with the bromine on a carbon touching three other carbons.

Treat both with hot water. The first one reacts slowly, and only if a nucleophile shoves the bromine off from the exact opposite side in one clean step. The second reacts fast — the bromine simply leaves on its own first, before anything else gets involved, forming a short-lived charged carbon in between.

Same halogen, same idea of "substitution," two genuinely different routes to get there. Telling them apart in advance — before running either reaction — is what the rest of this concept is for.
