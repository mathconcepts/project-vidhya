$\Delta H<0$ (exothermic) and $\Delta S<0$ (entropy decreases) is the one combination where spontaneity genuinely *depends on temperature*, in a direction many students get backwards.

$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$. With $\Delta S<0$, the term $-T\Delta S$ is *positive*, and it grows *larger* as $T$ rises — working harder against spontaneity at higher temperature, not less. A reaction with $\Delta H=-58$ kJ/mol, $\Delta S=-175$ J/(mol$\cdot$K) is spontaneous at $300$ K ($\Delta G=-5.5$ kJ/mol) but becomes non-spontaneous above $T = \Delta H/\Delta S \approx 331$ K — heating this particular reaction further eventually shuts it down, the opposite of the common intuition that "heat helps every reaction along".

The other three sign combinations of $\Delta H,\Delta S$ are each simpler: $\Delta H<0,\Delta S>0$ is always spontaneous; $\Delta H>0,\Delta S<0$ is never spontaneous; only $\Delta H,\Delta S$ sharing the same sign makes $T$ the deciding factor at all.
