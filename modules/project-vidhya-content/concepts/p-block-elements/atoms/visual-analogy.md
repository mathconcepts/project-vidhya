Picture two people trying to shake hands while each one is also holding three umbrellas close to their own body — the umbrellas keep bumping into each other before the hands can even get close. That is $F_2$: each fluorine atom holds three lone pairs (the umbrellas) so close to its small frame that the two atoms' lone pairs collide the moment the atoms try to bond (the handshake), weakening the bond itself. Chlorine atoms hold the same three "umbrellas" each, but chlorine is bigger, so the umbrellas from one atom do not reach far enough to jab the other atom's umbrellas — the handshake happens with less interference, and the bond ends up stronger despite chlorine's atoms being farther apart to begin with.

This is why fluorine, the smallest and supposedly "tightest-bonding" halogen, actually has a weaker bond than chlorine, and why it is dangerous in a different way — that weak bond breaks so easily that fluorine reacts almost explosively with nearly everything it touches, even though thermodynamically its bond looks feeble on paper.
