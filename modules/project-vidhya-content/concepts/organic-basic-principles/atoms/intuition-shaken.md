Compare two carbocations directly, one neighbouring carbon at a time.

First: $\text{CH}_3^+$ (a methyl cation). The positive carbon has zero neighbouring carbons, so zero neighbouring $\text{C-H}$ bonds can donate sideways into it. No help at all.

Second: $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+$ (a tert-butyl cation). The positive carbon has three neighbouring methyl carbons, giving nine $\text{C-H}$ bonds that can each overlap into the empty orbital, plus three separate carbons pushing electron density inward through the inductive effect.

Check: more donating neighbours reaching the same empty orbital means more stabilisation. Nine hyperconjugating bonds beats zero every time, so $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+$ sits far lower in energy than $\text{CH}_3^+$ — a conclusion reached by counting actual neighbours, not by memorising "tertiary beats primary" with no reason behind it.
