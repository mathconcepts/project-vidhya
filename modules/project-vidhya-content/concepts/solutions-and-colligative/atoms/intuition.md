Four properties of a solution are called **colligative**: relative lowering of vapour pressure, elevation of boiling point, depression of freezing point, and osmotic pressure. What makes them a family is that every one of them depends ONLY on the NUMBER of solute particles dissolved per unit of solvent — never on whether those particles are sugar molecules, salt ions, or anything else.

$\Delta T_b = iK_bm$ (boiling-point elevation) and $\Delta T_f=iK_fm$ (freezing-point depression) share the same structure: a solvent-specific constant ($K_b$ or $K_f$), the molality $m$ (moles of solute PER KILOGRAM of solvent — not per litre of solution), and the **van't Hoff factor** $i$.

$i$ is simply the actual number of particles a formula unit produces in solution, divided by the number of formula units dissolved. For a non-electrolyte like glucose, $i=1$ (one molecule, one particle). For a strong electrolyte like $\text{NaCl}$ that splits completely, $i$ equals the number of ions the formula gives — $i=2$ here. $i$ is the single number that carries "how many particles, really" into every colligative-property formula.
