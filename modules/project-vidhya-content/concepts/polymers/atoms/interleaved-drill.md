**Cross-concept check: polymers → hydrocarbons.**

**Question 1 (addition polymerisation needs a specific hydrocarbon feature):** Ethylene ($\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2$), propylene ($\text{CH}_3\text{-CH=CH}_2$), and styrene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-CH=CH}_2$) are all common addition-polymer monomers. What single structural feature do all three share, and why does that feature matter for addition polymerisation?

*Answer:* All three are alkenes — they carry at least one carbon-carbon double bond. Addition polymerisation works by that double bond opening up so one monomer links directly to the next, with nothing released. Without a double bond to open, there is no addition mechanism available at all — this is exactly why saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes, with no double bond anywhere) are never addition-polymer monomers.

**Question 2 (a monomer's carbon count carries straight through, unlike a condensation monomer's):** Polypropylene is made entirely from propylene monomers, $\text{CH}_3\text{-CH=CH}_2$ (three carbons each), by addition polymerisation. Does every carbon in the monomer end up in the final polymer chain?

*Answer:* Yes. Since nothing is released in addition polymerisation, every atom in propylene — all three carbons and all six hydrogens — ends up somewhere in the growing polypropylene chain. This is the direct opposite of a condensation polymer like nylon-6,6, where a water molecule (and the atoms in it) is deliberately lost at every single bond formed.

**Why this drill exists:** the double bond this concept relies on for addition polymerisation is the same carbon-carbon double bond the alkenes concept studies for its own reactions — recognising an alkene monomer here is the same skill as recognising an alkene substrate there.
