Picture nitrogen's lone pair as a hand reaching out to catch a proton. Water molecules crowd around that hand once it has caught the proton, holding on wherever there is an $\text{N-H}$ bond to grip — each remaining $\text{N-H}$ bond is one more place water can hold on and steady the catch.

A primary ammonium ion offers three such grips. A tertiary ammonium ion, with all three original hydrogens on nitrogen already swapped out for alkyl groups, offers only one. Fewer grips means a shakier, less stable catch — water simply cannot hold on as well, however strongly the alkyl groups themselves were pushing electron density onto that hand beforehand. This is exactly why a tertiary amine's real strength as a base, out in the open gas phase, is not fully carried through once water gets involved.

The same "how much can grip on, and how" idea reappears at the diazonium salt: it is a weak electrophile, a hand that can only just reach for electron density, and it needs an aromatic ring already loaded with extra electron density — pushed in by a group like $\text{-OH}$ — to have anything worth grabbing at all.
