A hospital ward has ten patients, each given a different starting dose of the same drug. Every patient's drug level traces a different curve over the next few hours — one starts high, one starts low — and yet, strip away the different starting doses, and all ten curves obey the exact same rule: the level falls at a rate proportional to how much is currently in the body. If $N(t)=N_0e^{-kt}$ describes patient number one with starting dose $N_0$, differentiating gives $\frac{dN}{dt}=-kN_0e^{-kt}=-kN$ — and the starting dose $N_0$ has vanished from that equation entirely. Ten different curves, one shared differential equation: this is what "forming" a differential equation from a family of curves means, and it works in reverse too.
