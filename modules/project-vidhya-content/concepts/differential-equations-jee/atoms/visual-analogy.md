Picture a family portrait of siblings: every one of them shares the same parents' features — same eyes, same jawline — but they differ in height, since each was born at a different time. The "shared features" are the differential equation: a rule every member of the family obeys. The "height, set by birth date" is the arbitrary constant: one number that varies from sibling to sibling and picks out which particular member of the family you are looking at.

Solving a differential equation is asking "what family does this rule describe?" — the answer is every possible sibling at once, the general solution. An initial condition is being told one sibling's exact height, which is enough to say exactly which sibling you are looking at, out of the whole family — the particular solution. "Forming" a differential equation runs the comparison backwards: given several siblings' photos (the family of curves), find the shared rule (the equation) they all obey, by noticing what stays fixed no matter which sibling you compare.
