**Classification**: **alkanes** ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$, only single bonds), **alkenes** ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n}$, one carbon-carbon double bond), **alkynes** ($\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n-2}$, one carbon-carbon triple bond).

**Conformations of alkanes**: rotation around a single $\text{C-C}$ bond gives different momentary shapes of the same molecule (not different molecules). The **staggered** conformation (bonds on adjacent carbons as far apart as possible) is lowest in energy; the **eclipsed** conformation (bonds lined up directly opposite each other) is highest, due to repulsion between the electron clouds of the eclipsing bonds.

**Markovnikov's rule**: when $\text{HX}$ (a hydrogen halide) adds across an unsymmetrical double bond, the hydrogen attaches to the carbon that already carries more hydrogens, and $\text{X}$ attaches to the more substituted carbon — because that pathway forms the more stable, more substituted carbocation intermediate first.

**Anti-Markovnikov (peroxide/Kharasch) effect**: with $\text{HBr}$ specifically, in the presence of peroxides, the addition reverses — bromine ends up on the *less* substituted carbon — because the mechanism switches entirely, going through a bromine free radical (not a carbocation) that adds to give the more stable radical intermediate. This reversal is observed only for $\text{HBr}$; $\text{HCl}$ and $\text{HI}$ do not show it, for bond-energy reasons specific to the radical steps involved.

**Ozonolysis**: ozone cleaves a carbon-carbon double bond completely, and each carbon becomes a carbonyl carbon (an aldehyde if it started with one hydrogen attached, a ketone if it started with two other carbon groups attached). Used to work out exactly where a double bond sat in an unknown alkene, by identifying the two carbonyl products.

**Aromaticity (Huckel's rule)**: a ring is aromatic only if it is cyclic, planar, fully conjugated (a p-orbital on every ring atom, with no gap), and carries exactly $4n+2$ pi electrons for a whole number $n$. A ring meeting the first three conditions but carrying exactly $4n$ pi electrons is **antiaromatic**, less stable than an equivalent open chain.

**Electrophilic aromatic substitution (EAS) directing effects**: substituents already on a benzene ring are classified as **activating, ortho/para-directing** (electron-donating overall, e.g. $-\text{OH}$, $-\text{NH}_2$, alkyl groups) or **deactivating, meta-directing** (electron-withdrawing overall, e.g. $-\text{NO}_2$, $-\text{COOH}$), based on whether they leave the ring richer or poorer in electron density, and on which ring positions that leftover density concentrates on. Halogens are the one common exception: deactivating overall, yet still ortho/para-directing.
