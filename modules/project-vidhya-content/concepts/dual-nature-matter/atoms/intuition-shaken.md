Take an electron, at rest, sped up through $100$ volts. Its momentum works out to $p=\sqrt{2meV}$, and plugging that in gives a **de Broglie wavelength** (the wavelength associated with a moving object) of $\lambda=h/p\approx 1.227$ angstrom, where one angstrom is $10^{-10}$ m — about the gap between atoms in a crystal.

Now compare a cricket ball of mass $0.16$ kg thrown at $30$ m/s. Its **momentum** (mass times velocity, $p=mv$) is $p=0.16\times30=4.8$ kg m/s. The same formula gives $\lambda=h/p\approx1.4\times10^{-34}$ m — far smaller than any atom, any nucleus, anything ever measured.

Same formula, $\lambda=h/p$, two wildly different answers: a crystal-sized wavelength for the electron, an undetectable one for the ball. Wave behaviour only shows up when momentum is small enough — for a cricket ball, it never is.
