Plot a complex number $z = a+bi$ as a point $(a,b)$ on a plane — the **Argand plane** — with the real part along the horizontal axis and the imaginary part along the vertical. Two things describe this point differently from ordinary $(x,y)$ coordinates: its **modulus** $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$, the straight-line distance from the origin (Pythagoras), and its **argument**, the angle the line to the point makes with the positive real axis, measured anticlockwise.

Writing $z$ using these two instead of $a$ and $b$ gives the **polar form**: $z = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)$, where $r=|z|$ and $\theta=\arg(z)$. This form is not just a different notation — it makes multiplication almost effortless: multiplying two complex numbers multiplies their moduli and adds their arguments. Multiply a number by itself repeatedly, and the argument just keeps adding to itself. That single fact — angles add under multiplication — is **De Moivre's theorem**, and it is also why the three **cube roots of unity** sit at $0°$, $120°$, and $240°$ around the unit circle: each is $120°$ apart, so tripling any of their angles brings you back to a multiple of $360°$.
