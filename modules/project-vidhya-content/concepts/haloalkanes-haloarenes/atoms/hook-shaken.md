Two real molecules, both bromides. First: 1-bromobutane, $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Br}$ — the bromine's carbon touches only one other carbon, a **primary** carbon.

Second: 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}$ — the bromine's carbon touches three other carbons, a **tertiary** carbon.

Add hot water to each. The first reacts slowly, only when a water molecule pushes the bromine off from directly the opposite side, in one single step.

The second reacts fast, and differently: the bromine leaves entirely on its own first, forming a charged carbon in between, before water ever gets close.

One halogen, two structures, two separate mechanisms — decided entirely by how many carbons crowd the one holding it.
