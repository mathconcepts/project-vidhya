**Cross-concept check: thermodynamics-chemistry → equilibrium-chemical.**

**Question 1 (Gibbs free energy sets the equilibrium constant):** A reaction has $\Delta G^\circ = -11.41$ kJ/mol at $T=298$ K. Using $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ (with $R=8.314$ J/(mol$\cdot$K)), is the equilibrium constant $K$ greater than or less than $1$?

*Answer:* Rearranging, $\ln K = \dfrac{-\Delta G^\circ}{RT} = \dfrac{11410}{8.314 \times 298} \approx 4.61$, so $K = e^{4.61} \approx 100$ — much greater than $1$. A negative $\Delta G^\circ$ (spontaneous in the forward direction, under standard conditions) always corresponds to $K>1$, meaning products are favoured at equilibrium.

**Question 2 (the sign link, without recomputing the exponential):** If instead $\Delta G^\circ$ were *positive*, would $K$ be greater than or less than $1$, and why?

*Answer:* Less than $1$. A positive $\Delta G^\circ$ makes $\ln K$ negative (from $\ln K = -\Delta G^\circ/RT$), and $\ln K < 0$ means $K<1$ — reactants are favoured at equilibrium. The sign of $\Delta G^\circ$ alone, without any further calculation, already says which side of the reaction equilibrium favours.

**Why this drill exists:** the equilibrium constant $K$ that chemical-equilibrium calculations use is not a separate, independently defined number — it comes directly from $\Delta G^\circ$, the exact same free-energy quantity spontaneity questions are built on.
