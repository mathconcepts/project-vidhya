**Problem:** Using Bohr's model, find the energy (in eV) of the photon emitted when an electron in a hydrogen atom falls from $n=2$ to $n=1$.

---

**Step 1 — Skip separately computing $E_1$ and $E_2$; use the combined formula.** $\Delta E = 13.6\left(\dfrac{1}{n_1^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_2^2}\right)$ eV, with $n_1=1$ (lower, final) and $n_2=2$ (higher, initial).

---

**Step 2 — Substitute directly.** $\Delta E = 13.6\left(\dfrac{1}{1} - \dfrac{1}{4}\right) = 13.6 \times \dfrac{3}{4} = 10.2$ eV.

$$\boxed{10.2 \text{ eV, emitted}}$$

---

**The distinction that actually costs marks: emission vs. absorption use the same formula with roles reversed.** For *absorption* (electron jumping $1 \to 2$), the energy *absorbed* is still $13.6\left(\dfrac{1}{1^2}-\dfrac{1}{2^2}\right)=10.2$ eV — identical magnitude, opposite physical direction. The formula's sign never distinguishes "absorbed" from "emitted" by itself; only which level the electron starts on and which it ends on does. A question stating "the atom absorbs a photon" and a question stating "the atom emits a photon" between the same two levels always share the same 10.2 eV magnitude — only the direction changes.
