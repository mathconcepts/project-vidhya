A molecule of 2-bromo-3-methylbutane, $\text{CH}_3\text{-CHBr-CH(CH}_3\text{)-CH}_3$, has its bromine sitting on a plain **secondary carbon** (a carbon joined to exactly two other carbons). Put it under conditions that let the carbon-bromine bond break on its own, and the textbook story says: bromide leaves, a **carbocation** forms right there (a carbon with only three bonds and a positive charge, one electron pair short), a nucleophile attacks it. Simple.

Except the major product often has the new group on a *different* carbon than the one that lost the bromine — as if the positive charge quietly moved sideways before anything else could reach it. Nothing outside the molecule pushed it there. The molecule rearranged itself, for a specific, checkable reason: a hydrogen atom's whole bonding electron pair slid over, carrying the positive charge to a carbon that could hold it more comfortably.

Knowing exactly when that move is worth making — and where the charge lands — is what the rest of this concept is for.
