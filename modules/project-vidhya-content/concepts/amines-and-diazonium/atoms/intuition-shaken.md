Nitrogen's lone pair grabs a proton — that's why any amine is basic at all. Now compare methylamine ($\text{CH}_3\text{NH}_2$, primary), dimethylamine ($\text{(CH}_3)_2\text{NH}$, secondary), and trimethylamine ($\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{N}$, tertiary).

**In the gas phase**, count only one thing: how many methyl groups are pushing electron density onto nitrogen. Trimethylamine has three, dimethylamine has two, methylamine has one. More methyl groups, more available lone pair, more basic. Order: tertiary > secondary > primary.

**In water**, count a second thing too: how many $\text{N-H}$ bonds are left on the ammonium ion once it grabs a proton, since water hydrogen-bonds to exactly those bonds. Protonated methylamine has three $\text{N-H}$ bonds. Protonated dimethylamine has two. Protonated trimethylamine has only one.

Fewer $\text{N-H}$ bonds means water stabilises that ammonium ion less well — and this effect is strong enough to drag trimethylamine's aqueous basicity down below dimethylamine's. Measured order in water: dimethylamine > methylamine > trimethylamine > ammonia. Same molecules, same lone pair, different answer — because water changes what's being counted.
