Think of DNA's two strands as two rows of a zip fastener, held together not by teeth but by hydrogen bonds between paired bases. Adenine and thymine always sit opposite each other, held by two hydrogen bonds — a slightly looser grip. Guanine and cytosine always sit opposite each other, held by three hydrogen bonds — a slightly tighter grip. A stretch of DNA rich in guanine-cytosine pairs needs more energy to pull the two strands apart than an equally long stretch rich in adenine-thymine pairs, purely because there are more hydrogen bonds per pair to break.

Proteins fold using a related but different picture: not two strands zipping together, but one long chain of amino acids folding back on itself, held in its folded shape by hydrogen bonds and other interactions between amino acids that end up near each other in three-dimensional space, even though they might sit far apart along the original sequence. Heat the protein enough, and those folding interactions break apart (denaturation) — the sequence of amino acids stays exactly the same, but the three-dimensional shape that made it functional is gone.
