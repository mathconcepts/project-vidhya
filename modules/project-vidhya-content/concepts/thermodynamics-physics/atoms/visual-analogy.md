Picture pressing the plunger of a syringe with its outlet sealed shut. Push it in fast, and the resistance climbs sharply the further in you push — the trapped air has nowhere to dump the heat building up inside it, so its pressure shoots up steeply for every extra bit the volume shrinks. Now imagine, instead, a syringe made of a material that lets heat leak straight through its walls, and you push it in slowly enough that heat keeps escaping the whole time. The resistance still rises as volume shrinks, but noticeably more gently, because escaping heat keeps the temperature (and so the pressure) from climbing as steeply.

On a graph of pressure against volume, starting from the same point and compressing to the same final volume, the fast, sealed push traces a **steeper** curve than the slow, leaky push — this is exactly the difference between an adiabatic curve ($PV^\gamma=\text{constant}$) and an isothermal curve ($PV=\text{constant}$) through the same starting point, since $\gamma>1$ always. A full pressure-volume cycle mixes several such curves end to end, which cannot be drawn as one honest single formula here, so this card describes the shape in words rather than showing a scene that would have to fake the missing pieces.
