**Problem:** 2-bromo-3-methylbutane ionises to a secondary carbocation at carbon 2. What happens next?

**Step 1.** Carbon 3, adjacent, is tertiary and carries one hydrogen. **Step 2.** That hydrogen's bonding pair shifts to carbon 2, moving the positive charge to carbon 3.

$$\boxed{\text{a hydride shift converts the secondary cation into the more stable tertiary one}}$$

---

**When it isn't a hydride that moves.** A hydride shift needs a hydrogen sitting on the neighbouring carbon to donate. If that neighbour is instead **fully substituted with carbons** — no hydrogen left to give — a whole **methyl group**, carbon and all, migrates the same way, carrying its bonding electron pair along.

This is not a separate fallback rule to memorise, but the identical logic — move to whichever neighbour yields the more stable cation — applied to whatever group that neighbour actually has available to donate. Checking only for hydrogens, and concluding "no shift is possible" when a methyl group could migrate instead, throws away a real answer.
