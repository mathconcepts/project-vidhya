Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is bent, with an angle of about $104.5°$ between its two O–H bonds — not a straight $180°$ line, even though oxygen has only two bonds to arrange. Why does adding two bonded atoms ever produce anything other than a straight line?

**VSEPR theory** (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) answers exactly this: it looks at *every* electron pair around the central atom, bonded or not, and pushes them as far apart as possible, since like-charged electron clouds repel. Oxygen in water has 2 bonding pairs *and* 2 lone pairs — four pairs total, arranged roughly tetrahedrally. The two lone pairs stay invisible in the final molecular shape (only atom positions are drawn), but they still take up room and squeeze the two O–H bonds together, bending the molecule.

Three separate questions — shape, orbital mixing, and bond stability — get three separate tools: VSEPR, hybridisation, and molecular orbital theory.
