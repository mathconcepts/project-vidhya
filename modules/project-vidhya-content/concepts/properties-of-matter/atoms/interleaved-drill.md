**Cross-concept check: properties-of-matter → work-energy-power.**

**Question 1 (elasticity, energy stored in a stretched wire):** A wire obeying Hooke's law needs a force $F=100\text{ N}$ to stretch it by $\Delta L=2\text{ mm}$. How much work is done stretching it, given that the force grows linearly from $0$ to $100\text{ N}$ over that stretch?

*Answer:* Since the force grows linearly (Hooke's law), the work done is the *area under the force-extension graph* — a triangle, not a rectangle: $W=\tfrac12 F\,\Delta L = \tfrac12(100)(0.002)=0.1\text{ J}$. This is the elastic potential energy stored in the wire.

**Question 2 (Bernoulli, restated as energy conservation):** Show that Bernoulli's equation $P+\tfrac12\rho v^2+\rho gh=\text{constant}$ is really conservation of mechanical energy, applied per unit volume of fluid.

*Answer:* Divide total mechanical energy by volume: $\tfrac12\rho v^2$ is kinetic energy per unit volume ($\tfrac12 mv^2$ divided by volume, with $\rho=m/V$), $\rho gh$ is gravitational potential energy per unit volume, and $P$ plays the role of "flow work" per unit volume done by the fluid behind pushing the fluid ahead. Their sum staying constant along a streamline is exactly $KE+PE=\text{constant}$, the same conservation law from work-energy-power, just written per unit volume instead of per unit mass.

**Why this drill exists:** a stretched wire storing work as elastic potential energy, and a flowing fluid trading pressure for speed, are both instances of the same accounting principle — energy put in has to show up somewhere, whether as $\tfrac12F\Delta L$ in a solid or as $\tfrac12\rho v^2$ in a fluid.
