**Problem:** Name $K_4[Fe(CN)_6]$, and state the one naming rule this example is built to test.

---

**Step 1 — Name the rule this problem is built to test.** When the complex ion is an ANION, the metal's name takes the $-ate$ ending, and for several metals (iron, copper, silver, gold, lead, tin) that means switching to the metal's Latin root, not just adding a suffix to the English name.

---

**Step 2 — Split the formula.** Complex anion: $[Fe(CN)_6]$. Counter-ion: $4$ potassium ions, $K^+$.

---

**Step 3 — Balance charge.** $4$ potassium ions contribute $+4$, so the complex anion is $-4$: $[Fe(CN)_6]^{4-}$.

---

**Step 4 — Find iron's oxidation state.** Each cyanido ligand ($CN^-$) carries $-1$; $6$ of them give $-6$. Iron's oxidation state plus $-6$ equals $-4$, so iron is $+2$.

---

**Step 5 — Name the ligand.** $6$ cyanido ligands become "hexacyanido".

---

**Step 6 — Name the metal, anionic form.** "Iron" becomes "ferrate" (from the Latin *ferrum*), not "ironate" — exactly the rule Step 1 named, and the detail a plain "-ate" suffix rule alone would miss.

---

**Step 7 — Assemble.** $\boxed{\text{potassium hexacyanidoferrate(II)}}$.
