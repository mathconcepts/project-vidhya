**Cross-concept check: mole-concept → thermodynamics-chemistry.**

**Question 1 (moles feed straight into a thermodynamics calculation):** Burning 2 g of hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$, molar mass 2 g/mol) releases 286 kJ of heat, given as the molar enthalpy of combustion. How much heat is released by burning 2 g of $\text{H}_2$?

*Answer:* Moles of $\text{H}_2 = 2 \div 2 = 1$ mol. The given 286 kJ is *per mole*, so 1 mol releases exactly $1 \times 286 = 286$ kJ. Every thermochemical value quoted "per mole" needs the actual moles present multiplied in — it is never applied directly to a mass.

**Question 2 (the same idea, with a mass that is not a whole number of moles):** Using the same molar enthalpy (286 kJ per mole of $\text{H}_2$), how much heat is released by burning 1 g of $\text{H}_2$?

*Answer:* Moles of $\text{H}_2 = 1 \div 2 = 0.5$ mol. Heat released $= 0.5 \times 286 = 143$ kJ — exactly half, because 1 g is exactly half of one mole of $\text{H}_2$.

**Why this drill exists:** every enthalpy value in thermodynamics is stated "per mole" of a specific substance, and that phrase is meaningless without correctly converting the actual given mass into moles first — the mole concept is the doorway every thermochemical calculation walks through before any energy number can be used.
