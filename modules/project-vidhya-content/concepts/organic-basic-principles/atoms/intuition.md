Three separate effects move electron density around a molecule, and mixing them up is the single biggest source of wrong stability rankings.

**Inductive effect**: electrons pulled (or pushed) along the chain of ordinary single (**sigma**) bonds, purely because some atoms hold shared electrons more tightly than others. It fades fast — noticeable one or two carbons away, essentially gone by the fourth.

**Resonance (mesomeric) effect**: electron density moving through a connected system of side-by-side **p-orbitals** (a **pi** system, or a lone pair sitting right next to one) — never through sigma bonds, and never to an atom outside that connected system. This is why an $-\text{OH}$ group attached to a benzene ring can push electron density all the way to a carbon three atoms away, something the inductive effect alone could never manage.

**Hyperconjugation**: a nearby carbon-hydrogen sigma bond's electron pair overlapping sideways with an empty (or partly empty) p-orbital right next to it — sometimes called "no-bond resonance," since no actual bond breaks in the process. More such $\text{C-H}$ bonds sitting beside a positive charge means more stabilisation reaching it, which is exactly why a carbocation with three neighbouring methyl groups (nine available $\text{C-H}$ bonds) sits far lower in energy than one with only a single neighbouring carbon.
