2-bromobutane, a secondary halide, is treated with sodium cyanide ($\text{NaCN}$, a good nucleophile that is a weak base) dissolved in DMSO (a polar aprotic solvent). Which mechanism operates, and what happens to the spatial arrangement at that carbon?

A) $S_N1$; the product is a racemic (roughly equal) mix of both spatial arrangements
B) $S_N2$; the product has the opposite (inverted) spatial arrangement from the starting material
C) $E2$; the product is an alkene, with no substitution at all
D) No reaction occurs, since secondary halides are unreactive to both substitution mechanisms
E) $S_N1$; the product keeps the same spatial arrangement as the starting material

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct answer: B) $S_N2$; the product has the opposite (inverted) spatial arrangement.**

A secondary carbon still has enough open space for backside attack, and a strong nucleophile (cyanide) that is a weak base, in a polar aprotic solvent that will not compete for it, is exactly the combination that favours $S_N2$. Backside attack flips the arrangement, giving inversion.

**Why the others fail:**
- A) and E) both assume $S_N1$, which needs a weak nucleophile and no strong base competing — cyanide is too good a nucleophile for the carbocation route to dominate here.
- C) would need a strong base, not a good nucleophile like cyanide.
- D) is wrong: secondary halides are reactive; they simply are not locked into one fixed mechanism the way primary and tertiary halides are.

</details>
