Two surprises sit inside this concept's name. First, the hook's own story: light, ordinarily a wave, behaves like a stream of particles called **photons** in the photoelectric effect — each photon strikes one electron and either carries enough energy $hf$ to free it, or does not, no matter how many dim photons pile up.

De Broglie ran the same surprise in reverse, for matter. If light can act like particles, he asked, why can't a moving object — an electron, a cricket ball, anything carrying **momentum** $p=mv$ (mass times velocity) — act like a wave? He proposed every moving object has a **de Broglie wavelength** $\lambda = h/p$.

For a cricket ball, $p$ is enormous, so $\lambda$ works out unimaginably tiny — no wave behaviour is ever seen. For an electron speeded up through a small voltage, $p$ is small enough that $\lambda$ lands where X-rays sit, and the wave side becomes measurable: such electrons genuinely diffract off a crystal, exactly the way light diffracts off a fine grating.

So the same constant $h$ that fixes a photon's energy ($E=hf$) also fixes a moving particle's wavelength ($\lambda=h/p$) — one number, running the duality in both directions at once.
