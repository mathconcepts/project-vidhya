Underneath both theories sits one shared fact neither one derives from scratch: how strongly a given ligand pulls electron density towards itself, ranked in an experimentally measured order called the spectrochemical series (roughly, from weak to strong: $I^- < Br^- < Cl^- < F^- < H_2O < NH_3 < en < CN^- < CO$). Both VBT and CFT borrow this same ranking, just translated into two different vocabularies.

In valence bond theory's language, a strong-field ligand like $CN^-$ can force pairing of the metal's own $d$ electrons, freeing up an inner $(n-1)d$ orbital for hybridisation — an "inner orbital complex" (e.g. $d^2sp^3$), usually diamagnetic or low-spin. A weak-field ligand like $H_2O$ cannot force that pairing, so the metal must reach for outer $nd$ orbitals instead — an "outer orbital complex" (e.g. $sp^3d^2$), usually more paramagnetic.

In crystal field theory's language, the exact same strong-field ligand splits the $d$ orbitals into two levels with a large energy gap, so electrons prefer to pair up in the lower level rather than pay the larger cost of jumping to the upper one — low spin. A weak-field ligand splits them only slightly, so electrons spread out unpaired across all orbitals first (Hund's rule) rather than pay even the smaller pairing cost — high spin. "Inner orbital, usually diamagnetic" and "low spin, small population of the upper level" are two names for the same underlying electron arrangement, arrived at through two different reasoning paths from the same spectrochemical-series fact.
