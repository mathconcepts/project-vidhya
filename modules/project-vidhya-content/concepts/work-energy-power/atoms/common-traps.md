- **Forgetting friction's work is negative by sign convention**: friction always opposes motion, so once you fix "direction of motion = positive", friction's force and its work must both be written negative. Dropping the sign turns a deceleration into an acceleration in the equations, even though the physical picture is obviously slowing down.

- **Assuming mechanical energy is conserved by default**: $KE+PE$ stays constant only when non-conservative forces (friction, drag, an applied push) do zero net work. The instant friction is mentioned in a problem, mechanical energy conservation is off the table — only the work-energy theorem (which always holds) can be trusted directly.

- **Using $W=Fd$ for a variable force**: this formula assumes $F$ is constant over the whole displacement. When force changes with position (a spring, for instance), work is the *area under the F-x graph*, or an integral — plugging in one value of $F$ and multiplying by total distance silently throws away everything the force does at other positions.

- **Treating "elastic" as "no energy lost anywhere"**: an elastic collision conserves kinetic energy specifically; energy in other forms (like potential energy stored briefly during contact) can still move around, as long as *total* kinetic energy before equals total kinetic energy after. Confusing this with "nothing changed at all" leads to wrong momentum-splitting between two colliding bodies.

- **Confusing power with force**: a heavier object needing more force to lift does not automatically mean more power — power also depends on how fast that lift happens ($P=Fv$). A slow crane and a fast crane can apply the exact same force and still have very different power ratings.
