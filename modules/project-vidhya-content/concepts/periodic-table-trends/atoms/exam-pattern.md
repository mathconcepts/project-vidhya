**How JEE actually asks this.**

- **MCQ: arrange-by-property questions built on the two named exceptions.** The moment a question lists elements including a Be/B pair or an N/O pair (or their Period 3 counterparts, Mg/Al and P/S) and asks you to order them by ionisation enthalpy or electron gain enthalpy, assume the exception is the whole point of the question — check subshell configuration before trusting left-to-right order.

- **MCQ: "which statement is incorrect" built on electron gain enthalpy.** A frequent wrong-option pattern claims fluorine has the most negative electron gain enthalpy in its group. It does not — chlorine's is more negative, because fluorine's small $2p$ subshell repels the incoming electron. Check the first-member-of-group exception before accepting any "most negative/most positive in the group" claim about F or O.

- **Trap: isoelectronic-ion radius questions.** A question giving $Na^+$, $Mg^{2+}$, $Al^{3+}$ (all 10 electrons) and asking which is largest is testing nuclear charge, not electron count — the answer is always the ion with the fewest protons among the set.

- **NAT/assertion-reason: diagonal relationship.** Expect a direct statement pairing Li with Mg, Be with Al, or B with Si, and a claim about one shared property (basicity of hydroxide, thermal stability of carbonate, covalent character). Check the specific property named — the diagonal relationship does not mean every property matches, only that charge-to-radius ratio is close.

- **Time budget:** an ionisation-enthalpy ordering question with 3–4 elements should take under 40 seconds if the exceptions are already memorised — write down the two "watch-out" configuration changes ($2s^2 \to 2p^1$, $2p^3 \to 2p^4$) mentally before comparing, rather than re-deriving effective nuclear charge from scratch each time.
