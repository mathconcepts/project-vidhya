Gravity's pull follows an **inverse-square law**: $F=GMm/r^2$, so doubling the distance $r$ cuts the force to a quarter, not a half. The **gravitational field** $g=GM/r^2$ is just this force per unit mass — the same inverse-square shrinkage applies to it directly.

**Gravitational potential energy** is defined as $U=-GMm/r$, taking $U=0$ at $r=\infty$ (infinitely far away, where gravity's pull has faded to nothing). The **negative sign is not optional** — it says gravity is always an attractive force pulling things together, so energy has to be *put in* to separate two masses to infinity, meaning any closer separation must sit at a lower (more negative) energy than that zero reference. The familiar $U=mgh$ from near-surface problems is only a **local approximation** to this, valid when $h$ is small compared to Earth's radius $R$ — using $mgh$ for a satellite hundreds of kilometres up quietly assumes $g$ stays constant, which it does not.

For a circular orbit, gravity itself supplies the centripetal force: $\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, which rearranges to $v=\sqrt{GM/r}$ — orbital speed falls as $r$ grows, exactly opposite to how total orbital energy behaves.
