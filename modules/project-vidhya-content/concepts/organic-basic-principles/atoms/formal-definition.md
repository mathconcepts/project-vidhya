**IUPAC nomenclature, in order**: (1) find the longest continuous chain containing the **principal characteristic group** — the highest-priority functional group present, which decides the name's suffix (e.g. $-\text{COOH}$ outranks $-\text{OH}$, which outranks a $\text{C=C}$ double bond); (2) number the chain to give that group the lowest possible locant; (3) if tied, give the lowest locants to substituents as a set; (4) if still tied, give the lowest locant to whichever substituent is cited first alphabetically.

**Structural isomerism** (same molecular formula, different connectivity): **chain isomerism** (a different carbon skeleton, e.g. a straight chain versus a branched one with the same formula); **position isomerism** (same skeleton and functional group, different location on it, e.g. propan-1-ol versus propan-2-ol); **functional isomerism** (an entirely different functional group, e.g. an alcohol and an ether sharing one formula); **metamerism** (same functional group, different alkyl groups on either side of it — only possible for a group with two attachment points, like an ether or an amine); and **tautomerism** (two isomers in rapid equilibrium through one moving hydrogen and a shifting double bond, e.g. a compound's keto and enol forms).

**Stereoisomerism** (same connectivity, different arrangement in space): **geometrical (cis-trans) isomerism**, possible only when a double bond or ring blocks free rotation and each of the two restricted carbons carries two different groups; **optical isomerism**, arising from a **chiral centre** (a carbon bonded to four different groups), giving two non-superimposable mirror-image forms.

**Electronic effects**: the **inductive effect** (a permanent pull or push of electrons through sigma bonds, weakening rapidly with distance); the **resonance (mesomeric) effect** (electron delocalisation through a conjugated pi system or an adjacent lone pair, either donating, $+M$, or withdrawing, $-M$); and **hyperconjugation** (donation from a $\text{C-H}$ or $\text{C-C}$ sigma bond, on a carbon next to an empty or partly-empty p-orbital, into that orbital).

**Reaction intermediates**: a **carbocation** (three bonds, $sp^2$, planar, electron-deficient — stabilised by nearby electron-donating effects); a **carbanion** (three bonds plus a lone pair, roughly pyramidal, electron-rich — stabilised by nearby electron-withdrawing effects); a **free radical** (an unpaired electron on carbon, stabilised the same way a carbocation is, since both have an incomplete orbital wanting extra electron density).

**Stability order — carbocations and radicals**: tertiary $>$ secondary $>$ primary $>$ methyl, because more neighbouring alkyl groups mean more hyperconjugation and more inductive donation into the electron-poor centre. **Carbanions reverse this order** — methyl is the most stable, tertiary the least — because alkyl groups' electron-donating push destabilises a centre that is already electron-rich.
