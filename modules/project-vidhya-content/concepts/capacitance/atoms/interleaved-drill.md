**Cross-concept check: capacitance → current-electricity.**

**Question 1 (energy still stored on a capacitor):** A $10\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor is charged to $5\ \text{V}$. How much energy does it store?

*Answer:* $U=\tfrac12CV^2=\tfrac12(10\times10^{-6})(5)^2=125\ \mu\text{J}$. A charged capacitor is, in this sense, exactly like a tiny battery — it has stored energy ready to be released.

**Question 2 (releasing that charge through a resistor):** This same charged capacitor is now connected across a $2000\ \Omega$ resistor, so it starts to discharge. What current flows the very instant the connection is made ($t=0$)?

*Answer:* At the very first instant, the capacitor's voltage hasn't had time to drop yet, so it briefly behaves exactly like a $5\ \text{V}$ battery driving current through the resistor: $I_0=\dfrac{V}{R}=\dfrac{5}{2000}=2.5\ \text{mA}$, straight from Ohm's law. Only after this instant does the current start shrinking as the capacitor's own charge (and voltage) runs down.

**Why this drill exists:** capacitance is often treated as a topic that ends the moment the plates are charged. In reality, the charge sitting there is exactly what current-electricity's next chapter releases and measures — the capacitor's stored $Q$ and $V$ are the starting conditions for a discharging current, not a separate, disconnected fact.
