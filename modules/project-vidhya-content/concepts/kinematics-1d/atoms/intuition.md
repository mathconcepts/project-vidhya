Motion along a straight line only ever needs three numbers at each instant: **displacement** $s$ (position measured from a fixed origin, along the chosen positive direction), **velocity** $v$ (the rate $s$ changes), and **acceleration** $a$ (the rate $v$ changes). Before writing a single equation, decide the origin and which direction counts as positive — the **sign convention**. Every $+$ or $-$ that appears afterward is meaningless without that choice made first.

Once acceleration is constant, three equations connect $s$, $u$ (initial velocity), $v$ (velocity at time $t$), $a$, and $t$: $v=u+at$, $s=ut+\tfrac{1}{2}at^2$, and $v^2=u^2+2as$. Each one is missing exactly one of the five quantities — picking the equation that already skips the quantity you don't know (and don't need) saves a step.

A **velocity-time graph** turns this into a picture: its slope at any point is the acceleration, and the *area* under it between two times is the displacement over that interval — a straight line for constant acceleration, with the area of the resulting triangle or trapezium giving displacement directly, no formula needed.

**Relative velocity** in one dimension is just subtraction done with signs respected: the velocity of $A$ as seen from $B$ is $v_A - v_B$, using the *same* sign convention for both.
