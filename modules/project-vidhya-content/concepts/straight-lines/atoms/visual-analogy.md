Picture two straight roads crossing somewhere on a map — any angle, anywhere, not necessarily at the map's origin. Multiply their two equations together: $(\text{road}_1)\times(\text{road}_2)=0$. The result is one single equation, degree two in $x$ and $y$. Any point sitting exactly ON road 1 makes that factor zero, so the whole product is zero too — same for road 2. So this ONE combined equation is secretly hiding two straight roads inside it, and $ax^2+2hxy+by^2+2gx+2fy+c=0$ is just what that multiplied-out picture looks like once the brackets are expanded.

But the resemblance only runs one way. Not every second-degree equation is two crossed roads in disguise — most of them are curved shapes instead, a circle or an ellipse, with no straight roads hiding inside at all. The condition $\Delta=abc+2fgh-af^2-bg^2-ch^2=0$ is the test for whether an equation is secretly two roads or genuinely one curved boundary; $h^2\ge ab$ on top of that says whether those two roads are real enough to actually drive on, or only exist on paper as a pair of imaginary lines.
