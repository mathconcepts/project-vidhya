Picture a circle of radius $a$, and a point walking around it at angle $\theta$, at position $(a\cos\theta,a\sin\theta)$. Now put a lamp far away and flatten that circle's shadow vertically — squash every height by a fixed factor $b/a$ — and the walking point's shadow lands at $(a\cos\theta,b\sin\theta)$: exactly a point on the ellipse $\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}=1$, at that same angle $\theta$.

That circle is the auxiliary circle, and $\theta$ is the eccentric angle — not the ordinary angle you'd measure from the ellipse's own centre to the point, but the angle on the UN-squashed circle that produces it. Both the circle-point and its ellipse-shadow always share the same $x$-coordinate, $a\cos\theta$; only the $y$-coordinate gets squashed, from $a\sin\theta$ down to $b\sin\theta$. This one relationship is what makes the whole parametric form $(a\cos\theta,b\sin\theta)$ trustworthy — it isn't a guessed formula, it is a literal flattened circle.
