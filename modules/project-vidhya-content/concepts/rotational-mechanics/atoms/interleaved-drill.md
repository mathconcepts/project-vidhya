**Cross-concept check: rotational-mechanics → gravitation.**

**Question 1 (spinning body):** A disc's moment of inertia drops from $I_1=6\text{ kg·m}^2$ to $I_2=2\text{ kg·m}^2$ with no external torque acting. If it started at $\omega_1=3\text{ rad/s}$, find $\omega_2$.

*Answer:* $L=I_1\omega_1=6\times3=18\text{ kg·m}^2\text{/s}$, conserved. $\omega_2 = L/I_2 = 18/2=9\text{ rad/s}$.

**Question 2 (orbiting planet):** A planet's distance from the Sun shrinks from $r_1=4\text{ units}$ to $r_2=1\text{ unit}$ at the nearest point of its elliptical path. If its speed at $r_1$ is $v_1=2\text{ units/s}$ (velocity perpendicular to the radius at that point), find its speed $v_2$ at $r_2$ using conservation of angular momentum.

*Answer:* For a point mass, $L=mvr$ (perpendicular component), conserved since gravity's pull is always along the radius and provides zero torque about the Sun. $v_1r_1 = v_2r_2 \Rightarrow (2)(4) = v_2(1) \Rightarrow v_2 = 8\text{ units/s}$.

**Why this drill exists:** a spinning disc's $L=I\omega$ and an orbiting planet's $L=mvr$ are the exact same conservation law, written for two different kinds of "spin" — one an object rotating on itself, one a point mass sweeping around a centre. Both hold because the torque about the relevant axis is zero: no external torque for the disc, a purely radial (zero-torque) force for the planet. This is exactly Kepler's second law in disguise — a planet moves fastest when closest to the Sun, for the same reason the skater spins fastest with arms pulled in.
