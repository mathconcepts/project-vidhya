An ideal gas undergoes an adiabatic process ($Q=0$) and does $300$ J of work while expanding. What is the change in its internal energy?

A) $\Delta U=+300$ J
B) $\Delta U=-300$ J
C) $\Delta U=0$
D) Cannot be found without knowing the temperature
E) $\Delta U=+300$ J only if the gas is monatomic

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct answer: B) $\Delta U=-300$ J.**

First law: $\Delta U=Q-W$. Here $Q=0$ and $W=+300$ J (the gas expands, doing positive work). So $\Delta U=0-300=-300$ J — the gas's internal energy *decreases*, since the energy for the work it did had nowhere to come from except its own stored internal energy.

**Why the others fail:**
- A) flips the sign — it treats work done ON the gas, not BY it, or forgets the minus sign in $\Delta U=Q-W$.
- C) wrongly assumes $Q=0$ means nothing changes at all; $Q=0$ only means no heat crossed the boundary, not that internal energy is unaffected.
- D) is wrong because the first law alone, with the two given numbers, is already sufficient — no temperature or gas type is needed to answer this specific question.
- E) wrongly makes the sign depend on the type of gas; $\Delta U=Q-W$ holds for any ideal gas, monatomic or not.

</details>
